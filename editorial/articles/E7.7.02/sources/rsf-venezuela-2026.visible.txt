After years of repression and strict control over information under Nicolas Maduro’s government, restrictions on the press and access to information were further exacerbated following the United States’ illegal military intervention in 2026. The country has been plunged into deep uncertainty surrounding the protection of press freedom, despite the release of detained journalists in early 2026.
Media landscape
An obscure policy for granting and revoking radio broadcast frequencies resulted in the closure of 200 radio stations. The Venezuelan government has also blocked online news content, thus impacting independent media sites. These blockages tend to be selective and intermittent, and accompanied by connectivity limitations that restrict the public’s access to independent news sources. Local organisations report that more than 400 media outlets were closed under Maduro’s government. Some independent media operate from exile or with teams outside the country to reduce risks and keep reporting.
Political context
President Nicolas Maduro continued the policy of “media hegemony” introduced by his predecessor, Hugo Chávez. Since the United States’ illegal military attack on 3 January 2026, which led to Maduro’s capture, the state of the country’s press freedom has been plunged into deep uncertainty, even though some detained journalists were released shortly afterwards.
Legal framework
Although freedom of expression is enshrined in Article 58 of the Constitution, there are no existing policies to protect it. Extravagant measures such as the “anti-hate” law have been approved by the National Constituent Assembly controlled by the Maduro government, which has illegally seized legislative powers. Some 80 Venezuelans, including 17 journalists and media outlets, have been sanctioned under this law, mainly for criticising influential people, especially on social media. Maduro’s regime has repeatedly invoked this law in its post-electoral repression and persecution of journalists. At the same time, regulatory frameworks governing funding increase the risk of administrative and criminal pressure being exerted on journalism supported by international cooperation.
Economic context
The economic crisis that took place between 2015 and 2020 led to a drastic reduction in the distribution of state advertising, the allocation of which is marked by opaque and arbitrary criteria. This benefits media outlets aligned with the government and forces others to broadcast official messages under threat of closure. The combination of economic suffocation, regulatory pressure and internet restrictions has pushed independent media outlets into financial instability and, in some cases, exile.
Sociocultural context
Venezuelan society has undergone a widespread crisis that has degraded living conditions. In response to the economic crisis, millions of citizens have emigrated to neighbouring countries. This migration also impacts the media, reinforcing the transnational circulation of media and content.
Safety
Journalism is heavily restricted in Venezuela. Reporters are often beaten or threatened in the course of their work. Under President Maduro’s control, neither the Attorney General’s Office nor the ombudsman’s office helped guarantee the safety of journalists, and physical and verbal violence against them is seldom investigated. The post-electoral repression in 2024 and arrests of media professionals were justified by the Attorney General’s Office, and the ombudsman remained silent about these abuses.
