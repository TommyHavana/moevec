For clarity, the strengths of each system 
have been formulated from the responses to 
questions which are highly weighted within 
each sub-index:
•	 A1 — minimum level of support for the 
poorest older individuals
•	 A2 — net replacement rate across a range 
of income earners
•	 S1 — coverage of employees in 
occupational pension schemes
•	 S2 — level of pension assets as a 
percentage of GDP
•	 Regulation and governance questions in 
aggregate
•	 Protection and communication questions 
in aggregate
A system has been described as “strong” 
in the above aspects if its score was 8/10 
or above. A system has been described as 
“good” in the above aspects if its score was 
6/10 or 7/10.

Argentina’s retirement income system is based primarily on a 
pay-as-you-go public social security regime, complemented by 
limited voluntary retirement savings arrangements, which may be 
sponsored by employers or individuals through insurance-based or 
other long-term savings vehicles.
The Argentinian system provides a good minimum level of support 
for the poorest older individuals, and a strong net replacement rate 
across a range of income earners. 
The overall index value for the Argentinian system could potentially 
be increased by:
•	 Developing a clearer framework for employer-sponsored 
supplementary retirement savings arrangements, potentially 
including automatic enrollment features and flexible 
contribution structures over time, to expand coverage and 
gradually increase long-term retirement savings 
•	 Promoting labor formalization and higher participation in the 
contributory system, thereby improving contribution density 
and reducing pressure on non-contributory and inclusion-based 
mechanisms 
•	 Establishing an appropriate legal and tax framework for 
supplementary employer-sponsored retirement plans, with 
the possibility of phased or default contribution levels as the 
system matures 
•	 Aligning access conditions for private retirement savings with 
retirement objectives, including the introduction of minimum 
preservation or access ages where appropriate
•	 Strengthening the regulatory framework for private retirement 
savings arrangements, including governance standards, 
protection, and communication to members
The Argentinian index value decreased from 45.9 in 
2025 to 43.7 in 2026, due primarily to a decrease in the 
minimum pension as a proportion of average wage as 
published by the OECD.

Australia’s retirement income system comprises a means-tested 
age pension (paid from general government revenue); a mandatory 
employer contribution paid into private-sector superannuation 
arrangements; and additional voluntary contributions from 
employers, employees, or the self-employed paid into the same 
private-sector plans. 
The Australian system provides a strong minimum level of support 
for the poorest older individuals, and has strong coverage of 
all employees in superannuation schemes and a strong level of 
pension assets as a percentage of GDP. Further, the system has 
strong regulation and governance along with protection and 
communication to members. 
The overall index value for the Australian system could potentially 
be increased by:
•	 Focusing on retirement income as the primary purpose  
of superannuation.
•	 Moderating the assets test on the means-tested age pension to 
increase the net replacement rate for average-income earners 
taking minimum government drawdown.
•	 Raising the level of household savings and reducing the level of 
household debt
The Australian index value increased from 77.6 in 2025 
to 80.6 in 2026, due in the main to an increase in the net 
pension replacement rates at retirement as published by 
the OECD.

Austria’s retirement income system is a multi-pillar system 
designed to provide retirement income through a combination 
of public, occupational, and private pension schemes. Pensions 
include a mandatory pay-as-you-go system, employer-sponsored 
pension schemes, and voluntary individual savings and investment 
plans, such as private pension insurance or savings accounts. 
The mandatory pay-as-you-go system is funded by social security 
contributions from both employees and employers, with benefits 
primarily based on the length of contribution periods and earnings 
history. Employer-sponsored pension schemes are often voluntary 
but are increasingly encouraged through legal frameworks  
and incentives. 
The Austrian system provides a strong minimum level of support 
for the poorest older individuals and a strong net replacement rate 
across a range of income earners. Further, the system has strong 
regulation and governance along with good protection of and 
communication to members.
The overall index value for the Austrian system could potentially  
be increased by:
•	 Increasing the minimum level of support for the poorest  
older individuals
•	 Expanding coverage of employees in occupational pension 
schemes, thereby increasing the level of contributions 
and assets (which could be done by collective bargaining 
agreements or tax-effective regulation)
•	 Introducing arrangements to protect the pension interests of 
both parties in a divorce
•	 Increasing the labor force participation rate at older ages
The Austrian index value increased from 54.5 in 2025 to 
56.9 in 2026, due mainly to the new question and revised 
weightings in the Integrity sub-index.

Botswana’s retirement income system consists of private and public 
pension systems. The Public Old Age Pension (POAP) is fully funded 
by the government and provides a benefit to all citizens above age 
65 living in Botswana. The benefit is adjusted periodically based 
on cost of living and is not means-tested. Private pensions consist 
of a mandatory pension scheme for public-sector employees and 
a voluntary pension system for private-sector employees. Private 
pensions include both DB and DC arrangements. 
The Botswanan system provides a good minimum level of support 
for the poorest older individuals. Further, the system has good 
regulation and governance along with strong protection of and 
communication to members.
The overall index value for the Botswanan system could potentially 
be increased by:
•	 Increasing the minimum level of support for the poorest  
older individuals
•	 Introducing mandatory contributions for private-sector workers 
in occupational schemes, to increase the net replacement rate 
for average-income earners
•	 Expanding coverage of employees in occupational pension

individuals transition into retirement
•	 Increasing the level of home ownership

rate pension supported by a means-tested income supplement, 
earnings-related pensions based on revalued lifetime earnings 
provided through a national program, voluntary occupational

retirement savings plans supported by favorable tax incentives.

occupational pension schemes (especially in the public sector), and 
a strong level of pension assets as a percentage of GDP. Further, 
the system has strong regulation and governance along with 
protection and communication to members.

Colombia’s retirement income system comprises a means-tested 
pension paid to those on the lowest incomes and two parallel and 
mutually exclusive pension systems. The first is a pay-as-you-go DB 
plan managed by a public-sector entity, and the second is a system 
of funded individual accounts offered through qualified financial 
institutions in the private sector. Individuals can make additional 
voluntary contributions to increase retirement benefits and/or 
reduce taxes. An employee elects to join one system, although 
there is the option to change later, with certain restrictions. The 
employer and employee contribution rates are the same for both 
systems, but retirement benefits differ between systems (DB versus 
individual accounts). 
The Colombian system provides a strong net replacement rate 
across a range of income earners. Further, the system has good 
regulation and governance along with good protection of and 
communication to members.

The Dominican Republic’s retirement income system is multi-pillar. 
It comprises a state-financed non-contributory solidarity pension, 
some public-sector DB pension arrangements, and mandatory, 
fully funded DC individual accounts. The total mandatory pension 
contribution is 9.97% of contributable salary, of which 8.40% is 
credited to the individual account. The normal pension age under 
the main contributory scheme is 60.
The Dominican Republic’s system provides a good minimum level 
of support for the poorest older individuals. Further, the funded 
pension pillar has good regulation and governance along with 
strong protection of and communication to members.
The overall index value for the Dominican Republic’s system could 
potentially be increased by:
•	 Increasing effective pension coverage and contribution density, 
particularly among self-employed, informal and intermittently 
contributing workers (while avoiding additional disincentives 
to formal employment), thereby increasing the level of 
contributions and pension plan assets
•	 Reviewing long qualifying periods and sharp eligibility 
thresholds so that workers with incomplete contribution 
histories can accrue more proportionate pension protection
•	 Gradually increasing the normal pension age for future cohorts 
as life expectancy rises, supported by measures that extend 
covered employment and contributions at older ages
•	 Broadening the investable universe and supporting prudent 
portfolio diversification, including greater exposure to  
growth assets.
The Dominican Republic’s index value for 2026 is 61.4.
