## Abstract

The aim of this article is to explore intersections between the ways different economies create new temporalities and how the need to navigate them affects the everyday lives of Cubans. Local understandings of work, technical and ethical approaches to time all play a role in the perception of marginality. As such, I explore how the predominant reliance on social networks and alternative means of subsistence affects work practices and more broadly defines daily realities of habaneros. I look closely at vernacular language that is used to describe economic practices, perceptions of the Cuban state and its presence in everyday economies. The article is based on a 9‐month ethnographic fieldwork dedicated to investigating everyday life of Havana’s informal settlements (llega y pon) and grassroots strategies of overcoming marginalization.

## Resumen

El objetivo de este artículo es analizar las intersecciones entre las formas en que las diferentes economías crean nuevas temporalidades y cómo la necesidad de transitar por ellas afecta a la vida cotidiana de los cubanos. La comprensión local de los enfoques laborales, técnicos y éticos sobre el tiempo influyen en la percepción de la marginalidad. De este modo, se explora cómo la dependencia predominante de las redes sociales y los medios alternativos de subsistencia afecta a las prácticas laborales y define más ampliamente las realidades cotidianas de los habaneros. El artículo se fija en el lenguaje vernáculo utilizado para describir las prácticas económicas, las percepciones del estado cubano y su presencia en las economías cotidianas. El estudio se basa en un trabajo de campo etnográfico de 9 meses dedicado a investigar la vida cotidiana de los asentamientos informales de La Habana (llega y pon) y las estrategias de base para superar la marginación.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

It was a rather slow and sunny March Saturday in Los Ríos, in the western part of Havana. As the temperatures started increasing, most of the people in the neighbourhood were hiding under the little shade they could find. I went on to the informal settlement and stopped by Aymaris’s house. To enter, I had to step above a bowl of apples, a fruit usually considered a delicacy in Cuba. Aymaris bought the apples the day before in Vedado district and was trying to sell them for 20 pesos each to make some profit. On the floor there were four puppies lying down being fed – the mother looked ill. They were only 17 days old, and the plan was to sell them as well, as these were the most popular pets in Havana in recent years.

Aymaris herself seemed a little agitated; she was suffering from a headache but had no medicine to treat it with. She was hoping to buy a blister of ibuprofen from the neighbour who brought a bag of different medicines from abroad and was selling them.cite5†^{1} As Aymaris was short on money, she was hoping to use the profit from the apples to get the pills and some fish from another street vendor who passed by with what he had fished that day. While trying to sell the apples, Aymaris was cleaning the house, handling a settlement of debt between her partner and a neighbour, and preparing food for her children.

It is not an uncommon thing in Havana to encounter someone visiting or selling products from house to house. Whether in informal settlements or touristic centres, there are always neighbours who can stop by or street vendors; sometimes, instead of knocking on doors, they will just shout announcing whatever they are selling. These vendors are perhaps the most tangible example of Cuba’s shadow economy – although out in the open, most of these activities are formally illegal, but to an extent permitted by the state and perpetuated by everyday practices. Cuban black market constructed through the resale of products, recycling or reusing certain materials such as perfume bottles, and the sale of contraband brought from abroad, medicine, clothes and foodstuffs, complements the official state economy.

The aim of this article is to explore intersections between the ways different economies create new temporalities and how the need to navigate them affects the everyday lives of Cubans. In the first section, I focus on the transformations of Cuban economies and analyse how local understandings of work and technical regimes of time evolved as part of the adjustments to the economic crisis that hit Cuba in the 1990s and its long‐term consequences. In the second part, I look closely at the vernacular language used to describe economic practices and alternative strategies of subsistence. I relate this language to changing moralities and the development of different ethics of time that define the everyday lives of Cubans who live from informal work, and analyse how it reflects perceptions of the Cuban state and its presence in everyday economies.

The article is based on 9‐month ethnographic fieldwork realized during three research trips between 2018 and 2020 dedicated to investigating everyday life of Havana’s informal settlements (llega y pon) and grassroots strategies of overcoming marginalization. During this time, I conducted participant observations of the daily life in the informal settlements and during a variety of activities conducted by community projects that work with marginalized communities across Havana. I also conducted in‐depth interviews with inhabitants of the settlements and project leaders.

## 2. TIMESCAPES OF A TRANSITIONING STATE

Cuban temporalities are particularly interesting as they merge apparently contradictory systems and somehow allow for their coexistence. Ways in which these temporalities emerge from economic practices, informal economies and ambiguities of the Cuban state can reveal different forms of engagement with and outside of the state. To achieve this, I will be drawing from Laura Bear’s (2016, 2017) concept of timescapes, with a particular emphasis on phronesis, the ethics of time and, to a lesser extent, techne, time and its measurement as a technique for capitalist production. Katherine Verdery (1996, p. 37) argues that it was the inability to maintain different temporalities that brought about the fall of the Soviet Bloc in Eastern Europe: ‘the fall of socialism was not simply in the intersection of two systems’ temporal cycles but rather in the collision of two differently constituted temporal orders, together with the notions of person and activity proper to them’. ## 7. RESOLVER AND AGENCY IN THE 'IDLE’ TIME

As 2018 Christmas was approaching, Ruxandra, a fellow researcher I shared my house with at that time, and I decided to invite friends over to our apartment and prepare Christmas dinner, garnering considerable interest as people wanted to see how these days are celebrated allá afuera (abroad); our initial plans were proving difficult to realize. I intended to make pierogi, a traditional Polish Christmas dish, but for that I needed flour, which was nowhere to be seen in the last 2 months; many pizzerias closed, and the bread sold at local bakeries started getting smaller. One day, I complained about this to Fara, to which she replied that I could have told her earlier as she recently had helped bakery workers to sell flour to a local pizzeria and she could have resuelto (resolved) some for me as well.

Resolver, to resolve, is another term that is commonly used to describe everyday realities in Havana. It closely resembles terms used in Eastern European countries during socialism such as 'załatwiać’ in Poland (Pieprzyca, 2018), and what it means is to acquire products by a variety of means, not necessarily through buying them (or not directly) but through the use of social networks, hierarchies, etc. ## 8. CONCLUSIONS

Coming back to Verdery’s claim on capitalist acceleration clashing with socialist economies, I believe that in Cuba this same acceleration profoundly transformed Cuban timescapes. The techniques of time, in the form of the rationing systems from a staple to assure equality, becomes a backup, a strategy for tricking time or an expression of care for those with a steady income. As salaried jobs within the state sector lost their value, the informal sector and entrepreneurship gained prominence. Both of them operate on immediacy, seizing opportunities as they occur and making the most out of them.

Cuba’s dual morality becomes reflected in the ethical uses of time; where self‐sacrifice and fixed work are not enough to survive and improve one’s situation, many choose invento as a means for survival and making ends meet. This, in convergence with a subsistence economy and constant shortages of products, raises the importance of social networks on which to rely to acquire goods. The uncertainty that marks Cuban temporalities is reflected in the vocabulary itself that refers to struggle, invention and resolving. Finally, time itself – manifested through the availability to put up with long lines and buy universally needed products in bulk to resell them – becomes a resource in itself. Many times, it is women, especially in the informal settlements, who take on the bulk of these changes, as not only do they need to navigate struggling for income but they are also responsible for the care work in their households. The ability to navigate these complex timescapes becomes thus an expression of agency and creative approaches to adapting to the difficult realities of a recurring crisis that became an everyday reality in Cuba.

In the end, those who rely on informal practices to make a living become ‘self‐regulating selves’ who, rather than being defined and adapting to the neoliberal work discipline, create a different form of flexibility. In the ‘covert capitalism’, this flexibility is defined by constant availability to identify and respond to emerging economic opportunities or difficulties. Thus, the division between work and free time loses its meaning as it is constantly redefined based on the circumstances.
