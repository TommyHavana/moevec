THE GLOBAL NETWORK OF  
CREDIT UNIONS AND  
FINANCIAL COOPERATIVES

The 2025 Statistical Report presents WOCCU’s best available 
picture of the global credit union and financial cooperative 
movement. This year’s report captures approximately  
420 million members across 93 countries and nearly  
US$4.0 trillion in assets – demonstrating the extraordinary 
scale of cooperative finance and its important role in  
financial systems around the world.
The report draws on data from national and regional credit 
union associations, regulators, central banks and other 
authoritative sources. Through WOCCU’s global network 
of 85 national and regional member organizations, we are 
also strengthening the relationships and data infrastructure 
needed to improve the quality, consistency and usefulness 
of global credit union statistics over time.
Our work is driven by three core commitments:
AMPLIFY ADVOCACY
Elevating member voices and strengthening influence in global 
policy and regulatory conversations.
DELIVER PREMIER MEMBERS SERVICES
Expanding education, connection, and shared learning to create 
more continuous engagement across the movement.
DEMONSTRATE IMPACT AND 
THE COOPERATIVE DIFFERENCE
Producing stronger evidence, insights and storytelling that show 
the value credit unions bring to members and communities.

To build that clearer picture, this year’s report reflects a 
significant effort to strengthen the underlying methodology of 
the Statistical Report. WOCCU reviewed country-level sources 
to identify the most authoritative and current “source of truth” 
available for each market. Where credible data permitted, 
reporting was expanded beyond affiliated institutions to capture 
the full national credit union or financial cooperative system.
This approach also required greater discipline about what is 
included. Outdated or unverifiable datasets were removed, 
known limitations were documented and methodological 
inconsistencies were corrected. As a result, changes in country 
or global totals should not always be interpreted as growth or 
contraction. In some cases, they reflect improved classification, 
broader national coverage or the removal of institutions that do 
not fit WOCCU’s working definition of a credit union or bona fide 
financial cooperative.
WOCCU has also begun mapping the data ecosystems that 
support credit union reporting across its global network. Preliminary 
research reveals considerable variation in who collects the data, 
how much of the system is covered, the level of detail reported and 
whether the information is publicly accessible. In some countries, 
standardized regulatory reporting provides comprehensive system-
wide information; in others, data remain divided among regulators, 
cooperative authorities and national associations or cover only 
affiliated institutions.
The result is a report that prioritizes consistency and credibility 
over the simple accumulation of institutions and assets. This 
work is producing a clearer picture of the global movement while 
helping WOCCU identify authoritative national sources, strengthen 
partnerships with data providers and recognize where stronger data 
systems are still needed.

A Global Movement with Distinct Regional Profiles
The global totals do not fully represent the differences in the scale and 
structure of credit union systems from one region to another. North 
America accounts for roughly three-quarters of reported global assets, 
reflecting the size of the United States and Canadian systems. Asia, 
Latin America and Africa, however, account for a substantial share of 
global membership and demonstrate the breadth of the cooperative 
model across very different economic and institutional environments.
These differences are important when interpreting the global figures. 
In some markets, credit unions are large, highly regulated financial 
institutions operating at national scale. In others, financial cooperatives 
are smaller community-based institutions whose importance is better 
understood through their reach, local presence and role in financial 
inclusion than through assets alone.

Where Global Financial Scale is Concentrated
Global credit union assets remain concentrated in a  
relatively small number of large, well-established systems.  
The United States is the largest, followed by Canada, Japan, 
Brazil, Australia, Thailand and South Korea. At the same time, 
the presence of major systems across North America,  
Asia-Pacific and Latin America illustrates that large-scale 
cooperative finance is not confined to a single regulatory or  
economic model.
The concentration of assets also reinforces the importance of 
looking beyond global totals. Smaller systems may represent 
a modest share of worldwide assets while serving a significant 
proportion of their national populations or providing financial 
services in communities and market segments that are 
otherwise underserved.

Perspectives from 
the Global Network
Beyond the numbers, perspectives from WOCCU’s Member 
organizations offer a snapshot of the forces shaping the 
future of cooperative finance. Responses were received from 
national and regional credit union associations across our 
global network. While the findings are directional rather than 
representative of every market, each respondent brings a 
system-wide perspective that offers valuable insight into 
the state of the global movement.
Across the responses, a consistent picture emerges: 
credit union leaders are navigating an increasingly 
complex environment while remaining focused on 
long-term opportunity. The findings point to four 
interconnected imperatives – building resilience against 
emerging risks, remaining relevant as technology and 
member expectations evolve, strengthening leadership 
and governance, and using collaboration to accelerate 
learning and shared solutions.

The Forces Shaping 
the Next Five Years
Regulation and digital competition stand out as 
the forces that the respondents expect to have 
the greatest impact on credit unions over the next 
five years. More than half identified regulation, 
while nearly half pointed to digital competition. 
Artificial intelligence and cybersecurity were each 
cited by more than one-third of respondents, 
underscoring how quickly technology is reshaping 
both opportunity and risk.
Taken together, the responses suggest that the 
next phase of credit union development will be 
shaped not by a single challenge, but by the 
interaction of regulation, competition, technology 
and changing expectations. Institutions will need 
the capacity to adapt while preserving the trust, 
member focus and cooperative identity that 
distinguish the model.
Strategic Priorities: 
Transformation, 
Growth and Relevance
Digital transformation is the clearest strategic priority 
across the survey responses. When respondents’ top 
three choices are weighted, digital transformation ranks 
well ahead of the other priorities, followed by growth 
in membership and assets, regulatory reform and 
increasing relevance to younger members.
The pattern reflects a movement focused 
simultaneously on modernization and growth. 
Technology is increasingly central to how credit unions 
compete and serve members, but transformation is not 
an end in itself: it is closely connected to expanding 
reach, responding to regulatory conditions and ensuring 
that the cooperative model remains relevant to the  
next generation.

Artificial 
Intelligence: 
Moving from 
Experimentation  
to Capability
Artificial intelligence is already moving from 
discussion to experimentation across the global 
network. Nearly half of respondents report that 
their organizations are experimenting with AI, 
and one-third are already using it for selected 
business functions. A smaller group reports that 
AI is part of organizational strategy or integrated 
across multiple areas, suggesting that adoption  
is advancing but remains at an early stage for 
many organizations.
Respondents also see considerable potential for 
AI to strengthen the movement. Member service 
and staff productivity emerge as the clearest 
opportunities, identified by 62% and 57% of 
respondents respectively. Data analytics and 
fraud detection follow, each selected by 43%. The 
results suggest that credit union leaders see AI 
not simply as a technology initiative, but as a tool 
for improving how institutions serve members, 
empower employees, use information and 
manage emerging risks.
The most important constraint appears to be 
organizational capacity rather than technology 
alone. Nearly two-thirds of respondents identify 
lack of expertise as the single greatest barrier 
to broader AI adoption – far more than any 
individual concern related to budget, regulation, 
privacy, leadership or the availability of  
suitable solutions.
Together, these findings suggest that the next 
challenge is not simply whether credit unions 
will use AI, but whether they can translate 
experimentation into practical applications 
while building the knowledge and institutional 
capabilities required to use it responsibly  
and effectively.

Leadership and 
Governance for a 
Changing Environment
The survey also points to a broadening governance agenda. 
Digital transformation is the most frequently cited single 
governance challenge, while cybersecurity, risk oversight 
and attracting younger directors follow closely. Looking 
ahead five years, regulation and AI rise further in importance 
as issues requiring board attention.
Governance is also a question of representation. For the 
first time, WOCCU examined the gender composition of 
leadership across a sample of its national and regional 
member organizations. Among 39 organizations for 
which complete board composition could be verified, 
women occupied approximately 28% of 406 board seats. 
Representation varied widely — from boards with no 
women to several that had achieved or exceeded gender 
parity. Across the broader sample for which leadership 
positions could be identified, women also accounted for 
approximately one-quarter of board chairs and just over 
one-fifth of chief executives.
The findings suggest that progress is uneven rather than 
uniform. Some parts of the global network demonstrate 
relatively balanced governance, while women remain 
significantly underrepresented in others. The variation 
points to opportunities for greater exchange across the 
movement around leadership development, succession 
planning and pathways to governance participation.

From Measurement to Insight
The Statistical Report provides more than an annual accounting of the global movement’s size. It is becoming a broader 
evidence base for understanding where cooperative finance stands, how it is changing and what may shape its future. 
More reliable country-level data helps WOCCU and its members demonstrate the movement’s scale and reach, while 
insights from member organizations bring emerging priorities, risks and opportunities into clearer focus.
The picture at year-end 2025 is one of scale, diversity and transition. Credit unions serve approximately 420 million 
members and manage nearly US$4 trillion in assets, even as the environment around them changes rapidly. Digital 
transformation, regulation, artificial intelligence, governance and competition are increasingly interconnected. The 
movement’s continued relevance will depend not only on the resilience of individual institutions, but also on its capacity 
to anticipate and respond to change, learn across borders, strengthen leadership and draw on the global cooperative 
network as a source of innovation and shared strength.
AI provides an early indication of how quickly 
governance agenda is evolving. While 23% of 
respondents report having an approved policy 
governing the use of AI, 45% report having no 
formal AI governance. Another 23% say AI has been 
discussed by their board without a formal policy, 
while 9% are currently 
developing one. The 
results suggest that 
governance frameworks 
are beginning to 
emerge, but in many 
organizations they 
have not yet caught 
up with the pace of 
experimentation and 
adoption.
There is no single governance challenge shared 
uniformly across markets. Instead, boards are 
being asked to oversee a wider range of strategic, 
technological and institutional issues at the same 
time. This reinforces the need for continuous 
director education, stronger succession planning 
and governance structures capable of responding  
to rapid change without losing sight of  
cooperative purpose.
