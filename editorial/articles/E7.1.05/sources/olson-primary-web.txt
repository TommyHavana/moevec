L156:     Introduction
L157:     It is often taken for granted, at least where economic objectives are
L158:     involved, that groups of individuals with common interests usually
L159:     attempt to further those common interests. Groups of individuals
L160:     with common interests are expected to act on behalf of their common
L161:     interests much as single individuals are often expected to act on
L162:     behalf of their personal interests. This opinion about group behavior
L163:     is frequently found not only in popular discussions but also in
L164:     scholarly writings. Many economists of diverse methodological and
L165:     ideological traditions have implicitly or explicitly accepted it. This
L166:     view has, for example, been important in many theories of labor
L167:     unions, in Marxian theories of class action, in concepts of &quot;countervailing power,u and in various discussions of economic institutions.
L168:     It has, in adClition, occupied a prominent place in political science,
L169:     at least in the United States, where the study of pressure groups has
L170:     been dominated by a celebrated &quot;group theoryU based on the idea
L171:     that groups will act when necessary to further their common or
L172:     group goals. Finally, it has played a significant role in many wellknown sociological studies.
L173:     The view that groups act to serve their interests presumably is
L174:     based upon the assumption that the individuals in groups act out
L175:     of self-interest. If the individuals in a group altruistically disregarded
L176:     their personal welfare, it would not be very likely that collectively
L177:     they would seek some selfish common or group objective. Such
L178:     altruism, is, however, considered exceptional, and self-interested behavior is usually thought to be the rule, at least when economic
L179:     issues are at stake; no one is surprised when individual businessmen
L180:     seek higher profits, when individual workers seek higher wages, or
L181:     when individual consumers seek lower prices. The idea that groups
L182:     tend to act in support of their group interests is supposed to follow
L183:     logically from this widely accepted premise of rational, self-interested
L184:     behavior. In other words, if the members of some group have a
L185:     common interest or objective, and if they would all be better off if
L186:     tnat objective were achieved, it has been thought to follow logically
L187:     that the individuals in that group would, if they were rational and
L188:     self...interested, act to achieve that objective.
L189:     But it is not in fact true that the idea that groups will act in their

L195:     in a group would gain if they achieved their group objective, that
L196:     they would act to achieve that objective, even if they were all rational
L197:     and self-interested. Indeed, unless the number of individuals in a
L198:     group is quite small, or unless there is coercion or some other special
L199:     device to make individuals act in their common interest, ra'tional,
L200:     self-interested individuals will not act to achieve their common or
L201:     group interests. In other words, even if all of the individuals in a
L202:     large group are rational and self-interested, and would gain if, as a
L203:     group, they acted to achieve their common interest or objective, they
L204:     will still not voluntarily act to achieve that common or group interest.
L205:     The notion that groups of individuals will act to achieve their common or group interests, far from being a logical implication of the
L206:     assumption that the individuals in a group will rationally further
L207:     their individual interests, is in fact inconsistent with that assumption.
L208:     This inconsistency will be explained in the following chapter.
L209:     If the members of a large group rationally seek to maximize their
L210:     personal welfare, they will not act to advance their common or
L211:     group objectives unless there is coercion to force them to do so, or
L212:     unless some separate incentive, distinct from the achievement of the
L213:     common or group interest, is offered to the members of the group
L214:     individually on the condition that they help bear the costs or burdens
L215:     involved in the achievement of the group objectives. Nor will such
L216:     large groups form organizations to further their common goals in
L217:     the absence of the coercion or the separate incentives just mentioned.
L218:     These points hold true even when there is unanimous agreement in a
L219:     group about the common good and the methods of achieving it.
L220:     The widespread view, common throughout the social sciences, that
L221:     groups tend to further their interests, is accordingly unj ustified, at
L222:     least when it is based, as it usually is, on the (sometimes implicit)
L223:     assumption that groups act in their self-interest because individuals
L224:     do. There is paradoxically the logical possibility that groups composed of either altruistic individuals or irrational individuals may
L225:     sometimes act in their common or group interests. But, as later,
L226:     empirical parts of this study will attempt to show, this logical possibility is usually of no practical importance. Thus the customary view
L227:     that groups of individuals with common interests tend to further
L228:     those common interests appears to have little if any merit.
