Foreword from Rhoshunda Kelly
Community bankers appreciate the recent relief in regulatory requirements—but are cautious 
they may be short-lived due to pendulum swings with changing administrations. 
Net interest margin pressure and core deposit growth are the top risks on their minds—again. 
The growth of the stablecoin market is fueling some of their concerns, due to potential siphoned 
deposits and uneven regulatory frameworks.
Meanwhile, rapidly changing technology is creating both opportunities and challenges. Many 
community banks have adopted or plan to adopt artificial intelligence in the next couple 
of years. 
At the same time, they continue to identify cybersecurity as their greatest internal risk, followed 
by the challenges and costs of implementing new technology.
In my 25 years of state supervision, I have always looked for emerging and reoccurring trends 
affecting financial institutions both in Mississippi and across the nation. Understanding the 
concerns of community banks helps craft policy so they can better serve their customers. That 
is why I always look forward to the Conference of State Bank Supervisors’ Annual Survey of 
Community Banks. For 13 years, the survey has provided policymakers with valuable insights 
into the challenges and opportunities community banks face.
The survey also highlights the resilience of community banks as they adapt to a changing 
financial landscape. That resilience underscores the vital role community banks play in 
supporting local businesses, serving consumers, and strengthening the communities they 
call home.
I invite you to read the full report to learn more about the issues community bankers identify as 
their most pressing concerns and greatest opportunities.
Rhoshunda Kelly
Chair, Conference of State Bank Supervisors Board of Directors 
Commissioner, Mississippi Department of Banking and Consumer Finance

2026 CSBS Annual Survey
Introduction
The 2026 Annual Survey of Community Banks (Annual Survey) 
continues to provide a comprehensive look at the evolution of 
community banking in the United States. The survey findings 
offer unique insights into the industry and provide perspectives 
that can influence future research and policy decisions. The survey 
would not be possible without the significant efforts of the state 
banking commissioners who actively promote it within their 
states. But ultimately, the value of the survey rests on the hundreds 
of community bankers who faithfully respond to it each year, thus 
promoting a clear and unvarnished view of the forces impacting 
their industry.
The Annual Survey, conducted by the Conference of State Bank 
Supervisors (CSBS), was administered from April 15, 2026, to  
July 15, 2026. The survey, which first launched in 2014, provides 
fresh insights into what community bankers view as the most 
significant opportunities and threats to their operations today and 
offers a glimpse into the issues they see emerging in the future.
The public release of the survey findings takes place at the start of 
the annual Community Banking Research Conference1 and has 
become a vital component of the research proceedings themselves, 
serving as a reference point throughout the event. The survey 
findings provide context for the research that is presented each 
year and for the other expert views that are offered over the two 
days of the conference.
As envisioned by CSBS more than a decade ago, the Annual 
Survey explores concepts that aren’t easily teased out of public 
banking data. Rather, the survey seeks to more thoroughly 
understand topics that aren’t routinely reported on through 
existing formal channels. In some cases, the survey simply 
seeks to “get under the hood” of some of the known data on 
community banks.
The survey is anonymous, although the survey design utilizes 
several mechanisms to prevent duplicate responses and to ensure 
that community banks—defined as institutions with less than  
$10 billion in total assets per the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform 
and Consumer Protection Act of 2010—are the only entities 
whose responses are captured.
By design, we repeat many questions each year to develop and 
maintain a consistent time series. Several other questions, however, 
are new, ensuring the survey explores emerging topics. 
The relevance of the survey is bolstered by this scalability and 
by its history of identifying issues that may become more 
prominent in the future. For example, the survey has given 
its readers a chance to see the evolution of bank and nonbank 
competition across different products and services categories. 
The evolution of fintech firms is a case in point: Community 
bankers initially viewed these firms as direct competitors, then 
as complementary and collaborative, and now as they are—fully 
integrated into all facets of the banking landscape, both as 
competitors and collaborators. 
A different, but arguably more impactful, technological force is 
once again impacting community banks and is highlighted across 
responses to the 2026 survey: artificial intelligence (AI). 
Unlike the community bank responses to the fintech questions 
in prior years, more than half of all respondents indicated in this 
year’s survey that they are already actively involved in some form 
of AI piloting. The speed of AI adoption seems to be accelerating. 
Understanding how community banks integrate AI into their 
business models, and the consequences of this integration, will be 
clear areas of interest in future surveys.
Beyond emerging issues, the survey also reminds readers that issues 
of the past can, and do, re-emerge. Take liquidity for example. 
Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, new fintech entrants, offering 
higher deposit rates, were seen as a potentially destabilizing force 
for the deposit franchises of banks, particularly community banks. 
The 2019 survey revealed significant banker concerns over deposit 
flight and the repricing of deposits and loans. These concerns even 
inspired a panel discussion during the 2019 Community Banking 
Research Conference on “The Future of Funding.”
Fast-forward to 2026, and the potential for deposit franchise 
erosion is once again sounding alarm bells among community 
banks—but this time due to the emergence of stablecoins. With 
the passage of the Guiding and Establishing National Innovation 
for U.S. Stablecoins (GENIUS) Act in July 2025, stablecoins have 
gained prominence as a competitor for deposits.
With full implementation of the GENIUS Act scheduled to 
take place in early 2027, the survey results reveal just how much 
community bankers are thinking about the potential impact of 
stablecoins as a future payment mechanism and highlight how 
much bankers are actively planning for a world in which stablecoins 
and tokenized deposits become mainstream. Some community 
bankers even indicated they are evaluating the extent to which their 
banks will actively engage in the issuance of stablecoins. 
It is clear in this year’s survey that community bankers will be 
carefully watching key developments around the final market 
structure framework for payment stablecoins and the rewards 
that stablecoin issuers are able to offer (and not offer) regarding 
their usage.
Competition and the impact of emerging technologies are just a 
few of the areas addressed in the 2026 survey. The survey dives 
into many other key areas of community banking, including 
internal and external risks, the mergers and acquisitions landscape, 
and the impact of inflation.

Key Findings
•	 For the second consecutive year, respondents identified net interest 
margins and core deposit growth as the top two external risks. 
Technology costs and the cost of funds ranked third and fourth.
•	 Concerns about regulation as an external risk continued to fall, with 
62% of respondents identifying regulation as either an “extremely 
important” or a “very important” area of concern, compared with 75% 
in last year’s survey, and 89% in the 2024 survey. 
•	 More than half of respondents indicated that they were unsure as 
to when inflation would return to the price stability target of the 
Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC), or that they expect it to 
occur beyond 2029. This represents a sizable increase from last year’s 
survey, when 30% of respondents were “unsure” or thought that the 
2% target would occur in “2028 or later.” 
•	 Cybersecurity was identified as the top internal risk for the ninth year 
in a row, followed by technology implementation and costs. Interest 
rate risk, which was called market risk in last year’s survey, rose from 
the lowest internal risk to the fifth highest internal risk this year, likely 
reflecting the shifting interest rate environment.
•	 More respondents have established relationships with fintech 
providers this year, with 33% of respondents utilizing fintechs for 
mobile banking support.
•	 Community banks continue to compete most intensely with one 
another for small-business, commercial real estate, and agricultural 
loans. Meanwhile, they compete most heavily with regional or 
national banks with a physical market presence for payment and 
wealth management/retirement services, and with credit unions for 
small-dollar unsecured loans.
•	 Banking market competition remained the primary challenge to 
retaining core deposits, followed by economic uncertainty. Fintech 
competition was the third cited risk, with 45% of respondents rating it 
as an “extremely important” or a “very important” challenge to their 
business, up from 34% last year.
•	 In response to an open-ended special question regarding regulatory 
changes and their impact over the past 12 months, bankers revealed a 
cautiously optimistic outlook. Most noted that overall regulatory relief 
was having a positive impact, although many expressed concern that 
such relief may be short-lived.
•	 In another special question, concerning the impact of stablecoin 
adoption on their banks, close to 60% of the respondents reported 
they saw deposit erosion and reduced lending capacity, and uneven 
regulatory implementation or regulatory arbitrage, as the “most 
impactful” or “moderately impactful” to their banks. 
•	 When asked about their current use of AI, about 53% of respondents 
indicated that they were piloting AI technology or engaging in limited 
use across specific functions. For those community bankers already 
engaged with AI, 77% said they are using it to assist with fraud 
detection and security.
Background
Prior to releasing the Annual Survey, CSBS and other key stakeholders 
identified relevant issues to include in the survey. Between April 
and July 2026, state banking agencies distributed the survey to 
community banks. This year, a total of 330 institutions responded.
Under the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection 
Act of 2010, a community bank is distinguished as having less 
than $10 billion in total assets. This report uses the Dodd-Frank 
benchmark to define community banks, with most respondents also 
being state-chartered institutions.
Limitations of this survey include, but are not limited to:
1.	 Not every U.S. state and territory had a community bank that 
completed the survey; in that case, that state or territory was 
not represented.
2.	 This survey was structured as a convenience sample, meaning 
respondents participated on a self-selected basis.
3.	 Respondents did not necessarily respond to every question.
4.	 Detailed statistical testing to demonstrate the 
representativeness of the surveyed banks of the overall industry 
was not performed.
Any conclusions gathered from this year’s survey should include 
these limitations accordingly. 
Since respondents were not required to answer every question, all 
responses are expressed as a percentage of total respondents to the 
respective question. Not all percentages will sum exactly to 100% 
due to rounding.

Similar to last year, net interest margins and core deposit growth ranked as the top two external risks faced by respondents in 2026. 
These results came as the Federal Reserve eased the federal funds rate by 75 basis points throughout late 2025 to a range of 3.50% to 
3.75%. Despite the lower federal funds rate, the net interest margin environment remained largely stable as deposit rates and loan rates

Banker Perspective 
“For a long time, it felt like nobody paid much attention 
to deposit rates, so, in a way, it’s been refreshing to start 
having conversations with our customers about this 
again. There’s a lot more competition and information 
out there. For example, a lot of our customers are using 
TreasuryDirect. Our bankers are trained to start proactive 
conversations on where the market is and what we can offer, 
and we empower them to negotiate on rates, especially 
when they’re working with a customer who has been with 
us for a long time. We’re also taking advantage of some of 
the new products out there. After the Silicon Valley Bank 
and Signature Bank failures, deposit insurance became a big 
topic. We’ve leveraged both ICS [Insured Cash Sweep] and 
CDARS [Certificate of Deposit Account Registry Service], 
both of which many of our customers use. It’s helpful to be 
able to compete with the big money-center banks on some 
of the products we offer, while giving people the option to 
keep their deposits local.”
—Scott Mizner 
American Bank, Bozeman, Montana
In the 2026 Annual Survey, about 80% of respondents identified 
net interest margins as either an “extremely important” or a 
“very important” risk to their business, down from 88% in last 
year’s survey, though it remains the leading external risk facing 
community bankers.
Core deposit growth remained the second most important 
external risk, with nearly 79% of respondents naming it as either 
“extremely important” or “very important,” which is unchanged 
from last year. Deposit capture from nonbanks, specifically, was 
highlighted as a concern in the open responses to this question 
and others throughout the survey.
Cost of technology and cost of funds rose to the third and fourth 
rankings, respectively, this year, supplanting economic conditions. 
Approximately 75% of bankers indicated that costs associated 
with technology and funding were either “extremely important”  
or “very important.”
Bankers expressed less concern about regulation, with 62% of 
respondents identifying regulation as either “extremely important” 
or “very important,” down from 75% last year, and 89% in 2024.

RISK OF INFLATION
The Federal Reserve targets 2% personal consumption expenditures (PCE) inflation as part of its dual mandate and closely monitors 
“core” PCE inflation, which removes the volatile components of food and energy. As of June 2026, the annual PCE inflation rate was 
3.7%, while core PCE inflation was 3.3%, measured on a 12-month basis. These rates are down from highs of 7.2% PCE and 5.6% core 
PCE in June 2022 and February 2022, respectively. 
With inflation still above the FOMC’s 2% price stability target, bankers were asked to rank the effects of inflation on their banks. Once 
again, community bankers cited costs of deposits as the most impacted, though the share of respondents who cited it as “most impactful” 
declined to 38% from 46% last year. Personnel expenses were again the second most impacted by inflation, according to 29% of 
respondents, up slightly from 23% last year. 
Close to 81% of respondents thought inflation-related challenges were likely to persist, up 6 percentage points from last year. 
However, respondents predominantly viewed inflation challenges as manageable—a positive sign for overall bank health. Only 2.7% of 
community bankers expected core inflation to return to the Federal Reserve’s target by the end of 2026, while 20% anticipated this to 
occur in 2027, 26% in 2028, and 23% in 2029 or later. About 28% reported they were “unsure.”

While every community bank faces unique risks, some themes carry across the industry and persist from year to year. Survey respondents 
again named cybersecurity and technology implementation and costs as the most important internal risks facing their institutions. 
Internal risk stemming from cybersecurity has held the top spot since being introduced in 2018. Credit and liquidity risks remained top 
of mind to bankers; however, the portion of respondents identifying liquidity risk as “extremely important” fell from 28% last year to 
20% this year. Interest rate risk management replaced market risk in this year’s survey and joined other internal risk categories as either 
“extremely important” or “very important” to the majority of respondents. Staff retention remained a critical internal risk, with more 
than 70% of respondents indicating it was either “extremely important” or “very important.”

Additional Internal Risks
•	 Staff retention increased to the fourth most prominent internal 
risk, with 71% of bankers naming it as either an “extremely” or 
a “very important” risk, up slightly from last year’s 68%. 
•	 Interest rate risk, previously identified as market risk, rose from 
the lowest internal risk to the fifth highest internal risk this year, 
likely reflecting the shifting interest rate environment.
•	 Consumer compliance and fair lending risk fell to the 
lowest-ranked internal risk this year, with only 41% of 
respondents designating it as “extremely important” or “very 
important,” down from 55% last year and 66% in 2024. 
•	 Artificial intelligence, fraud, and the ability to retain 
deposits were other internal risks bankers cited in their narrative 
responses to this particular survey question.
Online banking continued to be a key area of focus for respondents. Remote deposit capture and online bill pay (electronic bill 
payments) were offered by 89% and 81% of respondents, respectively. Additionally, 45% of respondents indicated that they currently 
offer and will continue to offer online loan applications, similar to last year’s results. 
Slightly more bankers than last year are receiving FedNow instant payments. More than 31% of bankers in this year’s survey 
indicated they don’t currently offer but plan to offer this service in the next 12 months, up from 25% last year. Around the same 
percentage of respondents indicated that they were sending FedNow instant payments compared to last year, with slightly more 
respondents planning to offer this service in the next 12 months.

Two new services were added to this survey question for this year: stablecoin services and tokenized deposits. Along with 
cryptocurrency services, all three had less than 2% of respondents noting that they currently offer and will continue to offer these 
services. Close to 18% of respondents indicated that they planned to offer tokenized deposits in the future, followed by 16% who 
plan to offer stablecoin services and 12% who plan to offer cryptocurrency services. There has been a steady, albeit slow, increase in 
respondents interested in offering cryptocurrency services over the last few years.
Finally, the share of bankers who currently offer and plan to continue to offer Small Business Administration loans declined to 60% 
from 66%, while those who plan to exit or substantially limit offering them next year rose to 5% from 2% in 2025.
Surveyed bankers were generally satisfied with the effectiveness of technology across all measured areas. More than 75% of respondents 
indicated that they were either “extremely satisfied” or “somewhat satisfied” with the effectiveness of their banks’ technology regarding 
asset liability management, interest rate risk, network service monitoring, and Bank Secrecy Act/anti-money laundering (BSA/AML).
Meanwhile, respondents expressed the lowest level of satisfaction in workflow processing and core service providers. For both 
technologies, more than half of respondents were either neutral or dissatisfied. This growing dissatisfaction with core service providers 
has been evident across several survey cycles.

The annual number of bank mergers increased by 38% in 2025, from 71 in 2024 to 98 in 2025. Bank M&A has been increasing since

Corporation and the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency. In May 2025, both agencies restored a more accommodative regulatory 
framework by rescinding policies from September 2024 that had increased the scrutiny of mergers.2
In the 2026 Annual Survey, 6% of respondents reported receiving and seriously considering an acquisition offer in the last 12 months. This 
number aligns with the results of the 2024 and 2023 surveys but represents a sharp decline from 2025, when 12% of respondents reported 
receiving or considering an offer. The excessive costs of doing business were cited as the top reason for considering offers this year, with 68% 
of respondents deeming these costs as “extremely important” or “very important” factors in why they would consider a merger, compared 
with 52% last year. Meanwhile, the costs of complying with regulations declined in importance, with the percentage of respondents 
declaring compliance costs either “extremely important” or “very important” falling to 47% from 58%. Succession planning remained the 
least-cited reason; however, year-over-year, the proportion of respondents that cited this factor as either “extremely important” or “very

participate in stablecoin networks, partner with existing issuers, or to provide custody services. Meanwhile, 37% indicated that they plan to 
participate in stablecoin networks through core service providers and 12% indicated they would partner with existing issuers to offer stablecoin 
services. Only 2% of respondents indicated that they would provide custody and compliance services for stablecoin issuers or customers.

Regarding barriers to AI adoption, 29% of respondents labeled cybersecurity concerns as the primary barrier to adoption, with others 
citing talent or expertise concerns and unclear return on investment. Other barriers included regulatory uncertainty, data limitations or

In this 13th Annual Survey of Community Banks, bankers shared perspectives on opportunities and risks facing their banks at a time of 
significant technological and regulatory change.
The rapid pace of technological innovation and adoption was discernable throughout the survey responses. Cybersecurity, for the ninth 
consecutive year, topped the list of internal risks facing community banks, followed by technology implementation and costs. Similar 
to prior years, cost or ability to implement technology remained the primary hindrance to adopting new technologies in the near term. 
Respondents again labeled cybersecurity risk as the most difficult challenge to implementing new technologies over the next five years. 
Several questions regarding stablecoins, tokenized deposits, and AI were added to this year’s survey. While most respondents indicated 
that they currently do not offer or plan to offer tokenized deposits or stablecoin services in the next 12 months, a small group of bankers 
do plan to offer one or both products in the near term. Of those survey respondents that plan to engage with stablecoins, a vast majority 
plan to participate in stablecoin networks through core service providers. A smaller proportion of respondents is planning to partner 
with existing issuers to offer stablecoin services. 
More than half of respondents are either planning to pilot AI or engage in limited use at their firms, while 18% are expecting to adopt 
some form of AI within the next two years. Of those bankers currently engaged with AI, the majority indicated they are using it to assist 
with fraud detection and security. When asked how they expect AI adoption to impact productivity over the next three to five years, 
respondents were nearly evenly split between “increase slightly” and “increase significantly.” Many bankers anticipate no net change in 
headcount due to AI adoption, although close to 20% said they expect employment to decline through attrition. 
As shown in recent readings of the CSBS Community Bank Sentiment Index, community bankers have become more optimistic about 
the regulatory climate. This sentiment was noted in both closed- and open-ended responses to this year’s survey questions. Concerns 
around regulation as an external risk, for instance, continued to fall in the rankings, with 62% of respondents identifying regulation 
as either an “extremely important” or a “very important” concern, a significant decline from 75% in last year’s survey and 89% in the 
2024 survey.

on the community bank business model. Historical survey results can be viewed on the CSBS website at csbs.org/survey. These results 
continue to demonstrate that community bankers remain resilient despite myriad challenges over the years. Through the 2008-09 
financial crisis, changing regulatory landscapes, and the 2019-21 pandemic, community banks have endured, serving their customer 
bases and remaining relevant in times of rapid change.

across your book? Are particular sectors, areas, or borrower 
types notably strong or weak? And where have you tightened or 
stepped back, if at all?
I’d say the economy across Texas, including El Paso and the 
Borderplex region in far west Texas, remains very strong. We’re not 
seeing credit issues in banking right now. Bankers in general had 
their best year ever in 2025, and the first six months of 2026 have 
been the best six months we’ve ever had.
If we aren’t careful, though, we could fall into the trap of thinking 
nothing can go wrong. The average American is still feeling very 
high inflation. Utility bills are up this summer. Insurance bills are 
up. Taxes are higher. Grocery and medical costs are up. At some 
point, consumers may begin to pull back. My concern is that we 
may be at a peak in spending. The question is: How long can it 
last? When will we start to see cracks in the economy or credit 
issues emerge? It doesn’t feel like it will happen this year, but it 
certainly could, especially based on what happens with rates.
The good news is that with our strong recent results, our capital 
positions and balance sheets are stronger than ever. Whenever the 
next downturn comes, we will be ready.
2. Core deposit growth and funding costs consistently rank 
among the top risks we hear from bankers. How is your bank 
competing for and retaining core deposits today? Where are you 
feeling pressure on pricing? What is working best? And are you 
worried about defending your deposit franchise? 
I’ve been in commercial banking for 43 years since I graduated from 
college in 1983. For most of my career, we told commercial bankers 
that their focus should be loan growth. Commissions, bonuses, 
and performance measurements were tied to growing loans, while 
deposits seemed to grow without much attention. That changed 
after the liquidity crisis, especially in 2023 with Silicon Valley Bank 
and the other failures. At WestStar today, our bonus structure for 
commercial bankers is essentially 50% based on loans and 50% 
based on deposits. For the first time in my bank’s history, we now 
have a deposit pipeline in addition to a loan pipeline.
The good news is that we’re focused on deposits. The bad news 
for us is that our competitors are too. In the past, we didn’t 
have the same level of competition from credit unions, private 
lenders, and others. While our rates need to be competitive, I do 
think community banks have an advantage because we know our 
borrowers and our communities. It helps to remind customers that 
if they keep deposits locally, those deposits can support job growth 
and economic growth in the community. If deposits leave for a 
national mutual fund company or national financial institution, 
that local impact is reduced. I respect those other institutions, but 
the more deposits we keep local, the more our towns can grow.
Community banking needs a story, and we need to stay close to 
depositors. When certificates of deposit mature, we need to call 
customers ahead of time because they know they can shop around. 
Deposit customers are much more educated than ever before. 
Whether they are small depositors, senior citizens, or businesses, 
they want to know what we will pay on deposits.
Deposits are now a central part of our internal conversations, and 
I do not see that changing anytime soon. If you don’t focus on 
deposits, you will run out of fuel.
3. Digital ledgering is receiving significant attention in 
Washington. Agencies have moved from frameworks to 
rulemaking, including proposed stablecoin rules and discussion 
of tokenized deposits. Do you see stablecoins and tokenized 
deposits as a genuine threat to your deposit base, or a distant 
concern? If the capability arrived through a core provider, 
banker’s bank, or consortium model, would your bank be 
interested? 
I do not expect every community bank to offer its own stablecoin, 
but we need to understand the space and determine who we can 
partner with, whether that is a larger regional bank, a national 
bank, or a large fintech that can help a community bank like mine 
move into this area. It would be a major mistake to assume this is 
going away.
Because we are on the border, cross-border payments are a 
particularly relevant use case. At banking conferences, trucking 
conferences, and car dealer conferences, people are talking about 
future payment systems. Businesses will ask what is better, cheaper, 
and faster.
A $3.3 billion community bank in El Paso cannot develop all of 
this on its own. We are operating on all cylinders, with record 
profits and strong capital, but if we celebrate that too much, 
we could be left behind on new technology. You’ve got to find

4. The regulatory and supervisory environment has shifted over 
the past year and a half. Agencies are rescinding policies and 
reopening long-standing rules, but many thresholds that weigh 
heavily on community banks were set decades ago. Are recent 
changes translating into real relief at your size, or are they more 
like window dressing? If you had a magic wand, which rules or 
thresholds would you target? And are you seeing any change in 
supervisory tone or experience? 
I think we’re at an exciting point. We’ve had a lot of regulatory 
relief, and it’s making a difference every day.
It’s important that bankers talk with our members of Congress 
about the value we provide. I’ve spent a lot of time with my 
congresswoman, Veronica Escobar. She’s been very good about 
asking questions about what works and what doesn’t, and I’ve been 
honest about my perspective as a banker. 
Regulation is important. I’ve got a 90-year-old customer with  
$1 million in the bank, and she wants that money available 
whenever she needs it. She trusts FDIC insurance and she trusts 
my bank. That trust is why we’re regulated. Even though things 
swung too far in the last administration, as bankers we don’t want 
to overcorrect with deregulation. We need to use common sense. 
Commissioner Charles Cooper and the Texas Department of 
Banking have been at the forefront of taking a measured approach 
to what works in our state. We want our industry to be safe.
As far as what I might change at the regulatory level, I’m most 
worried about crypto companies offering interest on funds held 
on their platforms. That could mislead consumers into thinking 
their funds are insured or protected in the same way bank 
deposits are. I don’t mind competing with crypto companies; 
they have an important role. But they do not lend money to 
small companies in our communities. The more deposits that 
move into that space, the less money that community banks 
have to lend back into local economies and support business 
growth and strong communities. 
There has also been a lot of fraud in parts of the crypto space, 
including crypto ATMs where fraudsters convince customers to 
withdraw cash and deposit it into a machine. Once the money 
is gone, customers often cannot recover it. Regulators require 
banks to do what’s right and treat people fairly. Consumers may 
not understand that some crypto companies aren’t held to the 
same standard.
5. Artificial intelligence (AI) is a major topic, especially as 
it relates to fraud. Bankers are optimistic about AI-driven 
efficiency but nervous about AI-enabled fraud, including 
synthetic voice fraud. One banker we spoke with last year had 
experienced a call center attack involving artificial calls. How 
are you using AI today, if at all? Has it delivered value for your 
institution? Is there potential for AI to help more than hurt? How 
has the fraud landscape changed? And are you worried about 
keeping pace?   
Any solid banker or institution needs to ask how AI can help, how 
to study it, and how it should enter the institution.
AI helps with fraud detection through both software systems 
and people. At the same time, fraudsters are using AI too, so it 
is helping both sides. It’s not stopping fraud, but it is helping us 
identify it. Fraudsters are also getting smarter and using AI tools 
they did not have before. When a customer gets a call that appears 
to come from the bank and shows our phone number, they may 
not know whether it is truly the bank. For the last 200 years, if the 
bank called, it was the bank. That is no longer always true.
At our annual shareholder meeting, and with our officers, I’ve 
said that I have about 350 employees, and I am not going to use 
AI to cut staff. However, I will use AI to become more efficient in 
certain areas, which allows us to redeploy people. The jobs those 
350 employees do today may look different in two, three, five, or 
10 years. If I can redeploy human capital into more revenue-driven 
and relationship-driven areas, that’s what I’m going to do. That 
will make the bank more efficient. I can grow to a larger size with 
the same number of employees, not fewer employees. Employees 
are understandably nervous, so it has been important to signal that 
no one is going to lose their job because of AI. Some employees 
will be retrained to do different things. 
We already use AI in loan approval software and in some Salesforce 
CRM tools. Our vendors have been helpful and ahead of the 
banking space in bringing community banks into AI. It would 
be very difficult for each community bank to develop its own AI 
tools. The largest banks can do that efficiently, but we will need to 
work with fintechs and vendors.
When I talk about technology now, I emphasize that people 
are more important than technology. We will all get access to 
technology, though some will get there faster than others. If you 
do not adopt it, you will not survive. But in the end, if many 
institutions have similar AI tools, the differentiator will be people. 
The best banks will be the ones with the best people. AI will not 
cure everything. Bad people using AI can create an even bigger 
mess than bad people without AI.
We also need to be very careful about protecting private customer 
information. Community banks are studying how to use AI 
without exposing customer information on the web or in the 
cloud. We want to use AI for prospecting, deposits, measurement, 
and analysis, but we need restrictions that prevent private 
information from being exposed. My biggest fear is that a bank 
will have a breach and need to notify regulators within about 
48 hours. That’s not good for your customers or anybody else,
