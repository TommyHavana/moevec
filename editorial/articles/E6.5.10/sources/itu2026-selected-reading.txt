 Foreword
The 2026 edition of Facts and Figures offers a fresh snapshot of global 
digital connectivity, highlighting both the progress achieved and the 
challenges that remain on the path towards universal and meaningful 
connectivity.
This year marks an important milestone: more than three-quarters of 
the world’s population are now online. An estimated 6.3 billion people 
use the Internet, up from 6.1 billion in 2025. Yet 2 billion people remain 
offline, reminding us that the benefits of digital connectivity are still 
far from universal.
Behind the global figures, significant divides persist. Internet use stands at 93 per cent in high-
income countries, compared with just 32 per cent in low-income countries. Women remain 
less likely than men to use the Internet, and substantial gaps persist between urban and rural 
populations. Although Internet use is growing relatively rapidly in least developed countries 
and landlocked developing countries, these countries continue to face considerable challenges.
There are also encouraging developments. 5G now covers almost six in ten people worldwide, 
while 97 per cent of the global population has access to a 3G or more advanced mobile network. 
Broadband services are also becoming more affordable. Notably, landlocked developing 
countries and Small Island Developing States have, for the first time, met the 2 per cent 
affordability target for data-only mobile broadband. 
These advances are essential, but connectivity is not an end in itself. It opens up opportunities 
in education, healthcare and economic development, and provides the backbone for emerging 
technologies such as artificial intelligence. Yet without adequate connectivity and digital skills, 
the benefits of these technologies risk remaining out of reach for many, reinforcing existing 
digital divides.
The findings also highlight the importance of better data. Reliable, timely and disaggregated 
statistics are essential for understanding digital divides and shaping effective policies. Yet 
fewer than half of countries have reported recent official data on Internet use, with even lower 
coverage for digital skills and mobile phone ownership. I therefore call on ITU Member States to 
strengthen the collection and reporting of ICT statistics, particularly through regular household 
surveys and timely, disaggregated data. 
The progress recorded in 2026 gives us reason for optimism, but also a clear call to action. 
We must accelerate our collective efforts to close the remaining digital divides and ensure that 
everyone, everywhere can benefit from meaningful connectivity.
Dr Cosmas Luckyson Zavazava 
Director of the Telecommunication Development Bureau 
International Telecommunication Union

 Methodology
For this publication, regional and global aggregates up to 2025 have been calculated using 
data supplied by Member States to ITU, supplemented where necessary by ITU estimates. 
Aggregates for those years may differ from those produced for earlier editions of Facts and 
Figures, as Member States may have submitted new or updated data. Except for price data, all 
2026 aggregates are ITU estimates computed using the methodology described below. For 
more detailed information, see Estimation methods for selected ICT indicators.
Percentage of population with mobile network coverage: end-2026 estimates
The percentage of the population covered by a mobile signal (2G, 3G, 4G, and 5G) refers to 
the percentage of inhabitants who have access to such coverage, regardless of whether they 
use the service. The indicator thus measures the availability of mobile cellular services, rather 
than the actual level of use or subscriptions. Data are disaggregated by urban and rural areas.
Coverage indicators are reported in aggregate form only (combining urban and rural coverage). 
As with many ICT indicators, ITU collects this data from telecommunication operators, 
telecommunication/ICT regulators and telecommunication/ICT ministries. This information 
is widely available from developed and developing countries alike. For countries that do not 
submit data, ITU applies two estimation methods: 1) estimation based on published data; and, 
if not feasible, 2) estimation based on trends.
Estimation based on published data
Data on coverage are sometimes available in the reports or on the websites of regulators or 
operators. This information can be used to estimate network coverage for the population of a 
country. The estimation process applies the following steps:
1.	
Identification of market players: First, it is necessary to determine how many mobile 
network operators (MNOs) and primary market providers offer the mobile service in the 
country, and obtain related information.
2.	
Annual report analysis: Once the MNOs are identified, their annual reports are 
downloaded from their websites and used for research. 
3.	
Alternative sources of business information: If operators do not publish annual reports, 
it may be possible to consult reports filed with national stock exchange commissions, or 
international exchange commissions (such as the Securities and Exchange Commission 
in the United States of America). These reports, which typically comprise Form 20-F and 
Form F-6 filings, provide comprehensive information about the company, including 
subscriptions, tariffs, staffing, financial data and more.
4.	
Press releases and other media reports: If it proves impossible to obtain data either 
from company annual reports or regulator publications, information can be approximated 
through industry analysis and news reports from trusted sources in the country, such as 
operator press releases, official statements from regulators or ministries, and media 
reports. Such data sources can provide absolute subscription numbers, market shares, 
penetration rates, growth rates and population coverage, which can be used to derive 
estimates for the country. 
Once data from all operators are available, the total percentage of the population covered can 
be calculated.

Estimation based on trends
When data are not available from filings or industry reports, it may be possible to produce 
estimates by analysing trends from the previous five years, using forecasting tools such as 
Expert Modeler in IBM SPSS.
As the data available for most countries do not differentiate between urban and rural coverage, 
they require disaggregation. This is a straightforward calculation, since mobile cellular coverage 
is virtually ubiquitous in urban areas and the proportion of urban to rural inhabitants is published 
by the World Bank.  Rural mobile population coverage is estimated by subtracting the urban 
population from the total population covered by a mobile signal, assuming urban areas are 
covered.
The percentage of the rural population covered by a mobile cellular signal (2G, 3G, 4G, and 
5G) is then obtained by dividing the number of rural inhabitants with coverage, by the total 
rural population and multiplying by 100. 
Aggregate values for regions, income groups and other groupings are calculated based on a 
weighted average of the values for individual countries. 
Internet use and mobile phone ownership estimates
Household surveys are a valuable source of statistics on Internet use and mobile phone 
ownership. Owing predominantly to their cost, however, relatively few countries administer 
such surveys and accordingly, there are large data gaps.
In addition, the delay between the collection of household survey data and their publication 
can be as much as two years or more, limiting their usefulness for ICT statistics given the rapid 
pace of technological change.
To overcome these shortcomings, data modelling and imputation are used to estimate missing 
historical values, and nowcasting techniques have been used to estimate the figures for 2026. 
The models used to estimate these missing values rely on a diverse range of widely available 
national indicators for mobile broadband subscriptions, ICT services affordability and GNI per 
capita, among others, and account for their changes over time. Weighted regression is used 
to give proportional influence in models to each region based on the number of countries in 
the region.
As well as official data from the membership, important sources used to obtain data and/
or cross-check estimates include the Gallup World Poll and the United Nations Children’s 
Fund household survey – the Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys. Additional data on socio-
demographic characteristics are obtained from the World Bank, the United Nations Educational, 
Scientific and Cultural Organization, the International Labour Organization, and the Population 
Division in the Department of Economic and Social Affairs of the United Nations (UN DESA). The 
2026 estimates have also implemented new urbanization data from the UN DESA Population 
Division, which now classifies the territory of countries into cities, towns and rural areas, rather 
than the previous urban/rural binary classification.7 The application of this new classification (with 
cities and towns assumed to comprise urban areas) has resulted in data that show a substantial 
decrease in the share of the world population residing in rural areas.
