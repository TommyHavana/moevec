L731@P20: Introduction
L732@P20: 
L733@P20: On December 11, 2008, the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) charged
L734@P20: Bernard L. Madoff (Madoff) with securities fraud for a multi-billion dollar Ponzi scheme
L735@P20: that he perpetrated on advisory clients of his firm. The complaint charged Madoff with
L736@P20: violations of the anti-fraud provisions of the Securities Act of 1933, the Securities
L737@P20: Exchange Act of 1934 and the Investment Advisers Act of 1940. In addition, the U.S.
L738@P20: Attorney’s Office in the Southern District of New York also indicted Madoff for criminal
L739@P20: offenses on the same date. On March 12, 2009, Madoff pled guilty to all charges and on
L740@P20: June 29, 2009, federal District Judge Denny Chin sentenced Madoff to serve 150 years in
L741@P20: prison, which was the maximum sentence allowed.
L742@P20: By mid-December 2008, the SEC learned that credible and specific allegations
L743@P20: regarding Madoff’s financial wrongdoing were repeatedly brought to the attention of
L744@P20: SEC staff, but were never recommended to the Commission for action. As a result, on
L745@P20: the late evening of December 16, 2008, former SEC Chairman Christopher Cox1
L746@P20: contacted the SEC Office of Inspector General (OIG) asking us to undertake an
L747@P20: investigation into allegations made to the SEC regarding Madoff, going back to at least
L748@P20: 1999, and the reasons that these allegations were found to be not credible. Former
L749@P20: Chairman Cox also asked that the OIG investigate the SEC’s internal policies that govern
L750@P20: when allegations of fraudulent activity should be brought to the Commission. In
L751@P20: addition, he requested that the OIG investigation include all staff contact and
L752@P20: relationships with the Madoff family and firm, and any impact such relationships had on
L753@P20: staff decisions regarding the firm.
L754@P20: The OIG’s investigation of the SEC’s failure to uncover Madoff’s Ponzi scheme
L755@P20: analyzed the SEC’s response to all complaints it received regarding the activities of
L756@P20: Madoff, and traced the path of these complaints through the Commission from inception,
L757@P20: reviewing what investigative or examination work was conducted with respect to these
L758@P20: allegations. We have also investigated the allegations of conflicts of interest regarding
L759@P20: relationships between any SEC officials or staff and members of the Madoff family,

L100: # Pyramid Schemes
L101: 
L102: In the classic "pyramid" scheme, participants attempt to make money solely by recruiting new participants, usually where:
L103: 
L104:   * The promoter promises a high return in a short period of time;
L105:   * No genuine product or service is actually sold; and
L106:   * The primary emphasis is on recruiting new participants.
L107: 
L108: All pyramid schemes eventually collapse, and most investors lose their money.
L109: 
L110: 【81†Image】
L111: Fraudsters frequently promote pyramid schemes through social media, Internet advertising, company websites, group presentations, conference calls, YouTube videos, and other means. Pyramid scheme promoters may go to great lengths to make the program look like a business, such as a legitimate multi-level marketing (MLM) program. But the fraudsters use money paid by new recruits to pay off earlier stage investors (usually recruits as well).
L112: At some point, the schemes get too big, the promoter cannot raise enough money from new investors to pay earlier investors, and people lose their money.
L113: These are some of the hallmarks of a pyramid scheme:
L114:   * Emphasis on recruiting. If a program focuses solely on recruiting others to join the program for a fee, it is likely a pyramid scheme. Be skeptical if you will receive more compensation for recruiting others than for product sales.
L115:   * No genuine product or service is sold.  Exercise caution if what is being sold as part of the business is hard to value, like so-called “tech” services or products such as mass-licensed e-books or online advertising on little-used websites. Some fraudsters choose fancy-sounding “products” to make it harder to prove the company is a bogus pyramid scheme.
L116:   * Promises of high returns in a short time period. Be skeptical of promises of fast cash – it could mean that commissions are being paid out of money from new recruits rather than revenue generated by product sales.
L117:   * Easy money or passive income.  There is no such thing as a free lunch. If you are offered compensation in exchange for doing little work such as making payments, recruiting others, or placing online advertisements on obscure websites, you may be part of an illegal pyramid scheme.
L118:   * No demonstrated revenue from retail sales.  Ask to see documents, such as financial statements audited by a certified public accountant (CPA), showing that the company generates revenue from selling its products or services to people outside the program.  As a general rule, legitimate MLM companies derive revenue primarily from selling products, not from recruiting members.
L119:   * Complex commission structure. Be concerned unless commissions are based on products or services that you or your recruits sell to people outside the program. If you do not understand how you will be compensated, be cautious.
L120: All Pyramid Schemes Collapse
L121: When fraudsters attempt to make money solely by recruiting new participants into a program, that is a pyramid scheme, and there is only one possible mathematical result – collapse. Imagine if one participant must find six other participants, who, in turn, must find six new recruits each. In only 11 layers of the “downline,” you would need more participants than the entire population of the United States to maintain the scheme.
