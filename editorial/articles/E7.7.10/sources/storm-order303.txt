KATHERINE POLK FAILLA, District Judge: 
The Court has reviewed the Government’s letter requesting that the 
Court issue an Order clarifying that it excluded time between April 24, 2026, 
and October 26, 2026, under 18 U.S.C. § 3161(h)(7) (Dkt. #301) and Defendant 
Roman Storm’s opposing request that the Court dismiss Counts One and Three 
with prejudice pursuant to 18 U.S.C. § 3162(a)(2) “because more than 70 
nonexcludable days have now elapsed on Mr. Storm’s Speedy Trial Act clock” 
(Dkt. #302).  For the reasons explained below, the Government’s request is 
granted, and Mr. Storm’s request is denied. 
A bit of context is in order:  Following a multiweek trial, on August 6, 
2025, the jury returned a partial verdict, convicting Mr. Storm on Count Two of 
the Superseding Indictment but failing to reach unanimity on Counts One and 
Three.  (Dkt. #224).  The Court declared a mistrial on those counts.  Thereafter, 
the parties promptly proposed a schedule for post-trial motions and agreed to 
exclude time under the Speedy Trial Act.  (Dkt. #226).  The Court adopted the 
parties’ proposed schedule and excluded time through December 18, 2025, the 
date for which it set a conference on the motions.  (Dkt. #227).  The conference

an Order setting the retrial date), and October 26, 2026 (the retrial date).  (Dkt.

Turning to the applicable law, it is clear that the Court may 
retrospectively clarify that it excluded time under the Speedy Trial Act so long 
as, “before granting the continuance,” it “implicitly made an ends-of-justice 
finding, [even] ‘if only in [her] mind.’”  United States v. Hoskins, 44 F.4th 140, 
152, 154 (2d Cir. 2022) (second alteration in original) (quoting Zedner v. United 
States, 547 U.S. 489, 506 (2006)).  The deadline to “mak[e] this finding explicit” 
is “‘by the time a district court rules on a defendant’s motion to dismiss under 
§ 3162(a)(2),’” id. at 154 (quoting Zedner, 547 U.S. at 507), or, in other words, 
at this very moment.  While the Court recognizes that retroactively making 
explicit exclusions of time that were previously only implicit is “disfavored” — 
and indeed, the Court shares this “prefer[ence]” for “contemporaneous findings 
on the record” — this practice is nonetheless permissible.  Id. 
Applying this standard, the Court denies Mr. Storm’s motion to dismiss 
because it is plain from the record that the Court made an implicit ends-of-
justice finding on April 24, 2026, excluding time from that date until the start 
of the retrial on October 26, 2026.  Previously, the Court had explicitly 
excluded time through the April 9, 2026 conference on Mr. Storm’s post-trial 
motion.  (Dkt. #278).  Following the conference, the Speedy Trial Act then 
automatically imposed a subsequent 30-day exclusion of time for a “delay 
reasonably attributable to any period … during which any proceeding

concerning the defendant is actually under advisement by the court.”  18 
U.S.C. § 3161(h)(1)(H).  Before that period could expire, the Court granted the 
Government’s request to schedule a retrial date, and, mindful of Mr. Storm’s 
concomitant request to provide him at least 90 days after resolution of his 
post-trial motion to prepare for retrial, the Court scheduled retrial to begin on a 
date well in excess of the Speedy Trial Act’s 70-day clock.  (Dkt. #288).  Implicit 
in the Court’s Order — as well as the Court’s adoption, two months later, of the 
parties’ jointly proposed pretrial briefing schedule — was the Court’s finding 
that the ends of justice were served by excluding time until the original retrial 
date of October 26, 2026.   
Moreover, the Court’s Order adjourning the retrial to April 26, 2027, also 
supports this inference.  There, the Court explained that it was excluding time 
to give the parties and the Court additional time to consider Mr. Storm’s post-
trial motion and to prepare for retrial.  (Dkt. #299).  The reasons that applied to 
the Rescheduling Order applied to the original Scheduling Order too.  The 
Court therefore clarifies that it excluded time between April 24, 2026, and 
October 26, 2026, under 18 U.S.C. § 3161(h)(7).1

Even if the Court had not implicitly made contemporaneous ends-of-justice findings 
and excluded time from April 24, 2026, to October 26, 2026, and instead were granting 
Mr. Storm’s motion to dismiss, it would only dismiss Counts One and Three without 
prejudice.  Weighing the relevant factors — “[i] the seriousness of the offense, [ii] the 
facts and circumstances leading to the dismissal of the charge, [iii] prejudice to the 
defendant, and [iv] the effect of re-prosecution on the administration of the Speedy Trial 
Act and of justice,” United States v. Teman, No. 19 Cr. 696 (PAE), 2019 WL 7041646, at 
*7 (S.D.N.Y. Dec. 20, 2019) — does not suggest otherwise, despite Mr. Storm’s 
arguments.  See also United States v. Hernandez, No. 19 Cr. 97 (VM), 2020 WL 4547900 
(S.D.N.Y. Aug. 5, 2020) (likewise weighing these factors and dismissing the indictment 
without prejudice); United States v. Mora, No. 04 Cr. 530 (LAP), 2005 WL 1354042 
(S.D.N.Y. June 7, 2005) (same).
