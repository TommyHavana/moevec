Abstract 
1 
The widespread use of digital technologies by young people has spurred speculation that their 
2 
regular use negatively impacts psychological well-being. Current empirical evidence 
3 
supporting this idea is largely based on secondary analyses of large-scale social datasets. 
4 
Though these datasets provide a valuable resource for highly powered investigations, their 
5 
many variables and observations are often explored with an analytic flexibility that marks small 
6 
effects as statistically significant, thereby leading to potential false positives and conflicting 
7 
results. Here we address these methodological challenges by applying Specification Curve 
8 
Analysis across three large-scale social datasets (ntot = 355,358) to rigorously examine 
9 
correlational evidence for digital technology affecting adolescents. The association we find 
10 
between digital technology use and adolescent well-being is negative but small, explaining at 
11 
most 0.4% of the variation in well-being. Taking the broader context of the data into account 
12 
suggests that these effects are too small to warrant policy change.

Re-Evaluating the Relation between Digital Technology Use and Adolescent Well-Being 
1 
 
2 
The idea that digital devices and the Internet have an enduring influence on how 
3 
humans develop, socialize, and thrive is a compelling one1. As the time young people spend 
4 
online has doubled in the past decade2, the debate about whether this shift negatively impacts 
5 
children and adolescents is becoming increasingly heated3. A number of professional and 
6 
governmental organizations have therefore called for more research into digital screen time4,5, 
7 
which has led to household panel surveys6,7 and large-scale social datasets adding measures 
8 
of digital technology use to those already assessing psychological well-being8. Unfortunately, 
9 
findings derived from the cross-sectional analysis of these datasets are conflicting; in some 
10 
cases negative associations between digital technology use and well-being are found9,10, often 
11 
receiving much attention even when correlations are small. Yet other results are mixed11 or 
12 
contest previously found negative effects when re-analysing identical data12. A high-quality 
13 
pre-registered analysis of UK adolescents found that moderate digital engagement does not 
14 
correlate with well-being, but very high levels of usage possibly has small negative 
15 
associations13,14.  
16 
There are at least three reasons why the inferences behavioural scientists draw from 
17 
large-scale datasets might produce divergent findings. First, these datasets are mostly 
18 
collected in collaboration with multidisciplinary research councils and are characterized by a 
19 
battery of items meant to be completed by postal survey, face-to-face or telephone interview6–
20 
8. Though research councils engage in public consultations15, the pre-tested or validated 
21 
scales common in clinical, social or personality psychology are often abbreviated or altered to 
22 
reduce participant burden 16,17. Scientists wishing to make inferences about digital 
23 
technology’s effects using these data need to make numerous decisions about how to analyse, 
24 
combine and interpret the measures. Taking advantage of these valuable datasets is therefore 
25 


PAGE 4

3
fraught with many subjective analytical decisions, which can lead to high numbers of 
1 
researcher degrees of freedom18. With nearly all decisions taken after the data are known, 
2 
they are not apparent to those reading the published paper highlighting only the final 
3 
analytical pathway19,20.  
4 
The second possible explanation for conflicting patterns of effects found in large-
5 
scale datasets is rooted in the scale of the data analysed. Compared to the laboratory- and 
6 
community-based samples typical of behavioural research (mostly < 1,000)21, large-scale 
7 
social datasets feature high numbers of participant observations (ranging from 5,000 to 
8 
5,000,000)6–8. This means very small covariations (e.g. r’s < .01) between self-report items 
9 
will result in compelling evidence for rejecting the null hypothesis at alpha levels typically 
10 
interpreted as statistically significant by behavioural scientists (i.e. p’s < .05). Thirdly, it is 
11 
important to note that most datasets are cross-sectional and therefore only provide 
12 
correlational evidence, making it difficult to pinpoint causes and effects. Thus, large-scale 
13 
datasets are simultaneously attractive and problematic for researchers, peer reviewers and the 
14 
public. They are a resource for testing behavioural theories at scale but are, at the same time, 
15 
inherently susceptive to false positives and significant-but-minute effects using the alpha 
16 
levels traditionally employed in behavioural science.  
17 
Given that digital technology’s impact on child well-being is a topic of widespread 
18 
scientific debate among those studying human behaviour22 and has real-world implications23, 
19 
it is important for researchers to make the most of existing large-scale dataset investments. 
20 
This makes it necessary to employ transparent and robust analytic practices, which recognize 
21 
that the measures of digital technology use and well-being in large-scale datasets may not be 
22 
well-matched to specific research questions. Further, behavioural scientists must be 
23 
transparent about how the hundreds of variables and many thousands of observations can 
24 
quickly branch out into ‘gardens of forking paths’19 with millions, and in some cases billions 
25

of analysis options. This risk is compounded by a reliance on statistical significance, i.e. 
1 
using p < .05, to demarcate “true” effects. Unfortunately the large number of participants in 
2 
these designs means small effects are easily publishable and, if positive, garner outsized press 
3 
and policy attention12. 
4 
Given that large-scale secondary datasets are increasingly available freely online, it is 
5 
not possible to convincingly document a scientist’s ignorance of the data before analysis24–26, 
6 
making hypothesis preregistration untenable as a general solution to the problem of 
7 
subjective analytical decisions. Specification Curve Analysis (SCA)27 provides a promising 
8 
alternative. Briefly, SCA is a tool for mapping the sum of theory-driven analytic decisions 
9 
that could have been justifiably taken when analysing quantitative data. Researchers 
10 
demarcate every possible analytical pathway and then calculate the results of each one. 
11 
Instead of reporting a handful of analyses in their paper, they report all results of all 
12 
theoretically defensible analyses (for previous examples see 27,28 and the Supplementary 
13 
Methods) 
14 
Given the substantial disagreements within the literature, the extent to which 
15 
children’s screen-time may actually be impacting their psychological well-being remains 
16 
unclear. The present research addresses this gap in our understanding by relying on large-
17 
scale data paired with a conservative analytic approach to provide a more definitive and 
18 
clearly contextualized test of the association between screen use and well-being.  
19 
To this end, three large-scale exemplar datasets (Monitoring the Future, Youth Risk 
20 
and Behaviour Survey and Millennium Cohort Study) from the US and the UK were selected 
21 
to highlight the particular strengths and weaknesses of drawing general inferences from large-
22 
scale social data and how they can be reconceptualised by SCA 6–8. Further, we tackle the 
23 
problem of significant-but-minimal effects in large-scale social data by using the abundance

4

Discussion 
19 
The possibility that adolescents’ digital technology use has a negative impact on 
20 
psychological well-being is an important question worthy of rigorous empirical testing. While 
21 
previous research in this area has equated findings derived from large-scale social data with 
22 
empirical robustness, the present research highlights deep-seated problems associated with 
23 
drawing strong inferences from such analyses. To provide a robust and transparent 
24 


PAGE 13
ADOLESCENT WELL-BEING AND DIGITAL TECHNOLOGY 
12 
investigation of the effect of digital technology use on adolescent well-being, we 
1 
implemented Specification Curve Analysis (SCA) with comparison specifications using three 
2 
large-scale datasets from the US and UK.  
3 
While we find that digital technology use has a small negative association with 
4 
adolescent well-being, this finding is best understood in terms of other human behaviours 
5 
captured in these large-scale social datasets. When viewed in the broader context of the data, 
6 
it becomes clear that the outsized weight given to digital screen time in scientific and public 
7 
discourse might not be merited on the basis of the available evidence. For example, in all 
8 
three datasets the effects of both smoking marijuana and bullying have much larger negative 
9 
associations with adolescent well-being (2.7x and 4.3x respectively for YRBS) than 
10 
technology use does. Positive antecedents of well-being are equally illustrative; simple 
11 
actions like getting enough sleep and regularly eating breakfast have much more positive 
12 
associations with well-being than the average impact of technology use (ranging from 1.7x to 
13 
44.2x more positive in all datasets). Neutral factors provide perhaps the most useful context 
14 
to judge technology engagement effects: the association of well-being with regularly eating 
15 
potatoes was nearly as negative as the association with technology use (0.9x, YRBS) and 
16 
wearing glasses was more negatively associated with well-being (1.5x, MCS). 
17 
With this in mind, the evidence simultaneously suggests technology effects might be 
18 
statistically significant but so minimal that they hold little practical value. The nuanced 
19 
picture these results provide are in line with previous psychological and epidemiological 
20 
research suggesting the associations between digital screen time and child outcomes are not 
21 
as simple as many might think11,13. This work therefore puts previous work that used the 
22 
YRBS and MTF to highlight technology use as a potential culprit for decreasing adolescent 
23 
well-being10 into perspective, showing the range of possible analytical results and comparison 
24 
specifications. The finding that the association between technology use and digital 
25 


PAGE 14

13 
engagement is much smaller than previously put forth has extensive implications for 
1 
stakeholders and policy-makers considering monetary investments into decreasing 
2 
technology use in order to increase adolescent well-being 29.  
3 
Importantly, the small negative associations diminish even further when proper and 
4 
pre-specified control variables, or caretaker responses about adolescent well-being, are 
5 
included in the analyses. This finding underlines the importance of considering high-quality 
6 
control variables, a priori specification of effect sizes of interest, and a critical evaluation of 
7 
the role that common method variance may play when mapping the effect of digital 
8 
technology use on adolescent well-being30. It is not enough to rely on statistical power to 
9 
improve scientific endeavour, large-scale social data analysis harbours its own challenges for 
10 
statistical inference and scientific progress.  
11 
This investigation therefore highlights two intrinsic problems confronting behavioural 
12 
scientists using large-scale social data. First, large numbers of ill-defined variables 
13 
necessitate researcher flexibility, potentially exacerbating the garden of forking paths 
14 
problem: for some datasets analysed there were more than a trillion different ways to 
15 
operationalize a simple regression19. Second, high numbers of observations render minutely 
16 
small associations significant through the default NHST lens31. With these challenges in 
17 
mind, our approach, grounded in SCA and including comparison specifications presents a 
18 
promising solution, so that behavioural scientists can build accurate and practically actionable 
19 
representations of effects found in large-scale datasets. Overall, the findings place popular 
20 
worries about the putative links between technology use and mental health indicators into 
21 
context. They underscore the need for open and impartial reporting of small correlations 
22 
derived from large-scale social data. 
23

14 
Our analyses, however, do not provide a definite answer to whether digital technology 
1 
impacts adolescent well-being. Firstly, it is important to note that using most large-scale 
2 
datasets one can only examine cross-sectional correlations links, and it is therefore unclear 
3 
what is driving effects where present. We know very little about whether more technology 
4 
use might cause lower well-being, whether lower well-being might cause more technology 
5 
use or whether a third confounding factor underlies both. As we are examining something 
6 
inherently complex, the likelihood of unaccounted factors affecting both technology use and 
7 
wellbeing is high. It is therefore possible that the associations we document, and those that 
8 
previous authors have documented, are spurious.  
9 
For the sake of simplicity and comparison, simple linear regressions were used in this 
10 
study, overlooking the fact that the relationship of interest is probably more complex, non-
11 
linear, or hierarchical13. Many measures used were also of low quality, non-normal, 
12 
heterogenous, or outdated, limiting the generalisability of the study’s inferences. As self-
13 
report digital technology measures are known to be noisy32, this could have also led to the 
14 
effects of technology on well-being being diminished due to low-quality measurement. 
15 
Lastly, we used NHST to interpret significance, which is problematic when using such 
16 
extensive data. To improve, partnerships between research councils and behavioural scientists 
17 
to better measurement, and pre-registering of analyses plans, will be crucial.  
18 
Whether they are collected as part of multi-lab projects or research council funded 
19 
cohort studies, large-scale social datasets are an increasingly important part of the research 
20 
infrastructure in the behavioural sciences. On balance, we are optimistic these investments 
21 
provide an invaluable tool for studying technology effects in young people. To realise this 
22 
promise, we firmly believe researchers must ground their work and debate in open and robust 
23 
practices. In the quest for high power, we caution scientists studying technology effects to 
24 
understand the intrinsic limitations of large-scale data and to implemented approaches that

1

Datasets and Participants 
7 
This paper’s analysis pipeline spans three nationally-representative datasets from the 
8 
US and the UK 6–8, encompassing a total of 355,358, predominately 12 to 18-year-old, 
9 
adolescents surveyed between the years of 2007 and 2016. These datasets were selected 
10

11 
use, and have been the focus of secondary data analysis to study digital technology 
12

13

14

15

16

17

18 
younger” to “18 years or older” (median = 16, sd = 1.24). The second U.S. dataset, 
19 
Monitoring the Future (MTF)6, launched in 1975 and is an annual nationally-representative 
20 
survey of approximately 50,000 American adolescents in grades 8, 10 and 12. While the 
21 
survey includes adolescents in grade 12, many of the key items of interest cannot be 
22 
correlated in their survey, and therefore their data was not included in our analysis. The 
23 
resulting sample from the MTF was collected from 2008 to 2016, and included 136,190 girls 
24

16 
and 132,482 boys, though the exact age of individual respondents was removed from the 
1

3 
prospective study collected in the U.K.; it follows a specific cohort of children born between 
4 
September 2000 and January 2001. We see this data as particularly high in quality due to its 
5 
inclusion of pre-tested measures and extensive documentation, highlighting good data 
6

7

Analytic Approach: Specification Curve Analysis 
24 


PAGE 21
ADOLESCENT WELL-BEING AND DIGITAL TECHNOLOGY 
20 
The study implements a Specification Curve Analysis examining the correlation 
1 
between our explanatory (digital technology engagement) and criterion variables 
2 
(psychological well-being) using the three-step SCA approach outlined by Simonsohn and 
3 
colleagues27 and applied in a recent paper by Rohrer and colleagues28. We add a fourth step 
4 
in order to aid interpretability of our results in the context of large-scale social data. Details 
5 
of the SCA method and the corresponding visualisations can be found in the Supplementary 
6 
Methods.  All the necessary code to reproduce these analyses can be found in the 
7 
Supplementary Software, for details see the Code Availability Statement at the end of the 
8 
paper. 
9 
Identifying Specifications. The first step taken was to identify all the possible 
10 
analysis pathways that could be used to relate technology use and adolescent well-being. Due 
11 
to the complexity of the original data we decided to use simple linear regression modelling to 
12 
draw inferences about technology associations, which left three key analytical decisions: (1) 
13 
How to measure well-being, (2) How to measure technology use, and (3) How to include 
14 
covariates (for details about these decisions, and others, see Table 1). 
15 
There are a wide variety of questions and questionnaires relating to well-being in each 
16 
dataset. Many of these items, even if partitioned questionnaires reflecting a specific construct, 
17 
have been selectively reported over the years. It is noteworthy that researchers have not been 
18 
consistent and have instead engaged in picking and choosing within and between 
19 
questionnaires (see Supplementary Table 6). These analytic decisions have produced many 
20 
different possibilities for combining and analysing these measures, making the pre-specified 
21 
constructs more of an accessory for publication than a guide for analyses. Any combination 
22 
of the mental health indicators is therefore included in the SCA: The measures by themselves, 
23 
the mean of the measures in pairs of two, the mean of the measures in threes etc. up to the 
24 
mean of all measures.  
25 


PAGE 22
ADOLESCENT WELL-BEING AND DIGITAL TECHNOLOGY 
21 
For MCS, we included a decision of whether to use well-being questions answered by 
1 
cohort members or those answered by their caregivers, we do not combine the two. For 
2 
YRBS we also included an additional analytical decision of whether to take the mean of the 
3 
five dichotomous well-being measures, or whether to code each participant as “1” who 
4 
answered yes to one or more of the questions, as this has been done in previous analyses of 
5 
the data10. The supplementary materials additionally present SCAs which include only pre-
6 
specified well-being questionnaires for MCS (Supplementary Figure 6), however these do not 
7 
allow comparisons of our SCAs to results of previous work that has selectively combined 
8 
questions from various datasets10. The next analytical decision is what technology use 
9 
variables to include, where we include all questions concerning technology use in the 
10 
questionnaires, and their mean, as done by previous studies10. The last analytic decision taken 
11 
is whether or not to include covariates in the models. Because of the sheer size of these 
12 
datasets there is a combinatorial explosion of different covariate combinations that could be 
13 
used in each regression. We therefore analysed regressions either without covariates or with a 
14 
pre-specified set of covariates based on a literature review concerning child well-being and 
15 
digital technology use11. 
16 
When examining the distributions of the data, many of the variables are highly 
17 
skewed (e.g. the 5-item technology use measures in MTF) or questionably linear (e.g. 3-item 
18 
happiness measure in MTF). We opted to treat these variables as continuous so that our 
19 
analyses and results would be directly comparable with those of previous studies10,33.  Data 
20 
distribution was assumed to be normal throughout the analysis but is not formally tested for 
21 
each specification. 
22 
Implementing Specifications. Next, for each specification defined we ran the 
23 
appropriate regression, and noted the standardised β of technology uses’ correlation with 
24 
psychological well-being, the corresponding two-sided p value and the partial 𝜂2 calculated 
25 


PAGE 23
ADOLESCENT WELL-BEING AND DIGITAL TECHNOLOGY 
22 
using the R heplots package. Listwise deletion for missing data was used as this is more 
1 
efficient in terms of computational time. This assumes that data is missing completely at 
2 
random, which could easily not be the case. For example, a child’s health, academic 
3 
performance or socioeconomic background could change its probability of completing the 
4 
questionnaire fully, and is likely to bias estimates. It is therefore important to note that this is 
5 
a potential source of bias, possibly changing the nature or strength of associations found.  
6 
To make the results easily interpretable, the specifications were ranked and plotted in 
7 
terms of ascending standardised β. The median standardised β of all the possible 
8 
specifications provides a general overview of the effect size. Below that plot, we also 
9 
indicated which set of analytical decisions led to what standardised β. This allows us to 
10 
visualise what analytical decisions influence the results of the SCA (more details of these 
11 
plots can be found in the Supplementary Methods).  
12 
Statistical Inferences. It is then possible to test whether, when considering all the 
13 
possible specifications, the results found are inconsistent with results when the null 
14 
hypothesis is true (i.e. that technology use and adolescent well-being are unrelated). To do so, 
15 
a bootstrapping technique put forth by Simonsohn et al.27 was implemented, creating data 
16 
where the null hypothesis is true by forcing the null on the data. To create this data, the beta-
17 
coefficient of the variable of interest from the full regression model multiplied by the x-
18 
variable (technology use) was subtracted from the y-variable (well-being). This created a new 
19 
set of data points that were then used as the new y-variable, creating datasets where the null 
20 
hypothesis was known to be true. Participants were then drawn at random – with replacement 
21 
– from this null dataset, creating bootstrapped null samples on which a new SCA model is 
22 
run. This was done 500 times. Once we obtained 500 bootstrapped SCAs, where we knew the 
23 
null hypothesis was true, we examined whether the median effect size in the original SCA 
24 
was significantly different to the median effect size in the bootstrapped SCAs. To do so, we 
25 


PAGE 24
ADOLESCENT WELL-BEING AND DIGITAL TECHNOLOGY 
23 
divided the number of bootstrapped datasets that have larger median effect sizes than the 
1 
original SCA by the total number of bootstraps to find the p value of this test. We repeat this 
2 
test focusing also on the share of results with the dominant sign, and also the share of 
3 
statistically significant results with the dominant sign23.  
4 
Comparison Specifications. Lastly, these analyses were supplemented with a 
5 
comparison specifications section, putting into context the effects found in the SCA. To do 
6 
so, we performed a literature review to choose four variables in each dataset that should be 
7 
positively correlated with psychological well-being, four variables that should be negatively 
8 
correlated with psychological well-being and four that should have no or little association 
9 
with psychological well-being. A SCA was run for each of the variables and the mean of the 
10 
technology use variables present in the dataset, graphing their specification curves. These 
11 
methods provide a way for researchers to transparently, openly and robustly analyse large-
12 
scale governmental datasets to produce research that accurately depicts associations found in 
13 
the data for both the academy and the public.
