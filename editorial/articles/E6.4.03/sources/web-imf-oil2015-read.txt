L21: ### Overview of oil price shock and broad effects
L22:   * World oil prices plunged from $105 to about $50 per barrel since mid-2014.
L23:   * Impact split by oil trade status:
L24:     * Oil-importing countries: faster growth, lower inflation, stronger external positions, and generally no significant fiscal pressures.
L25:     * Oil-exporting countries: slower growth, weaker external current account balances, and some will face fiscal pressures due to reliance on oil-related revenues.
L26:   * Venezuela is highlighted as facing the most precarious position, having had severe economic imbalances before the decline in oil prices.
L27: ### Pass-through to domestic prices and macroeconomic implications
L28:   * Many countries are allowing lower world oil prices to pass through into lower domestic energy costs, increasing disposable income for consumers and businesses via cheaper transportation and electricity.
L29:   * Effects of allowing pass-through:
L30:     * Supports growth.
L31:     * Eases inflationary pressures.
L32:     * Helps stabilize external current account balance by stimulating demand for non-oil imports, partly offsetting the decline in oil imports.
L33:   * Regional pass-through estimates by end 2015:
L34:     * 60 percent of countries (examples: Barbados, Costa Rica, Guatemala) will allow for a full pass through from world to domestic fuel prices.
L35:     * Less than 30 percent will not allow any pass through.
L36: ### Fiscal effects for net oil importers
L37:   * Channels through which world oil prices affect fiscal balances for net oil importers:
L38:     * Decline in the value of taxes on oil imports (value-added taxes and ad valorem import duties).
L39:     * Decline in the costs of fuel subsidies, especially where governments do not allow full pass through.
L40:   * Net effect:
L41:     * Fiscal position in most net oil importing countries would strengthen moderately due to the decline in world oil prices.
L42:     * Some improvements stem from governments not allowing full pass through of lower world oil prices into domestic fuel prices.
L43: ### Fiscal effects for net oil exporters
L44:   * Net oil exporters identified: Bolivia, Colombia, Ecuador, Mexico, Trinidad and Tobago, and Venezuela.
L45:   * Common fiscal channels in exporters:
L46:     * State-owned oil companies dominate the sector and provide fiscal revenues via income taxes, dividends, and royalties.
L47:     * These companies may monopolize domestic petroleum sales and bear costs of domestic fuel subsidies.
L48:   * Country-specific developments and implications:
L49:     * Ecuador, Mexico, and Trinidad and Tobago have begun to curb spending to offset the decline in hydrocarbons-related revenues.
L50:     * Mexico:
L51:     * Raising the domestic price of gasoline by 1.9 percent in 2015 to enhance revenues from domestic sales.
L52:     * Purchased insurance from the market that helped limit the decline in oil-related revenues in 2015; additional fiscal adjustment will be needed in 2016 because this insurance provided a hedge for just 2015.
L53:     * Colombia:
L54:     * Relies on a fiscal rule that spreads out the adjustment to shifts in world oil prices.
L55:     * Fiscal deficit will widen in 2015 with the fall in oil-related revenues.
L56:     * In coming years, the government will need to raise non-oil revenues to reach legal targets for the structural fiscal balance while protecting key expenditure programs.
L57:     * Bolivia:
L58:     * Will experience a significant loss in revenues because the price of its natural gas exports is linked to spot world oil prices.
L59:     * Government has built up considerable buffers of deposits and net international reserves to give itself room for maneuver in the near team.
L60:     * Venezuela:
L61:     * Public sector derives a large share of fiscal revenues from oil exports; fiscal position will suffer the most from the decline in world oil prices.
L62:     * Domestic price of gasoline is expected to remain close to zero, virtually eliminating potential revenues from domestic sales.
L63:     * Fiscal difficulties in Venezuela could create pressures on Petrocaribe recipient countries; in many of these countries the decline in the value of oil imports exceeds projected financing from Petrocaribe.
L64:     * Many Petrocaribe recipient countries use the subsidy component to support long-term spending and could face difficult fiscal adjustment if Petrocaribe financing were cut.
L65: ### Policy implications and recommendations
L66:   * General assessment:
L67:     * The new outlook for world oil prices will benefit some countries and present challenges for others.
L68:     * Overall, this outlook does not pose a threat to macroeconomic stability in the region because most countries will continue to maintain sound policy frameworks.
L69:     * Venezuela is a notable exception where significant loss in oil export revenues will aggravate an already tenuous situation.
L70:   * Recommended policy actions:
L71:     * Use the drop in oil prices as an opportunity to eliminate poorly targeted subsidies.
L72:     * Establish pricing mechanisms that allow for automatic adjustment of domestic prices to changes in international fuel prices.
L73:     * Diversify sources of fiscal revenue to avoid overreliance on fiscal income from either oil exports or imports.
