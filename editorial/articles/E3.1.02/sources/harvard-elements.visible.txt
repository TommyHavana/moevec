What Is Negotiation? Understanding the Seven Elements of Successful Negotiation
Learn the building blocks of indispensable negotiation business skills.
By
Katie Shonk
— on
October 28th, 2026
/
Negotiation Skills
Comment
In both our personal and professional lives, we negotiate constantly—sometimes formally, sometimes informally. In a given day, we might:
Discuss the terms of a job offer
Bargain over the price of a car
Finalize a contract with a supplier
Try to persuade a child to eat their vegetables
Manage a workplace conflict
Convince a client to accept a delayed delivery
“Like it or not, you are a negotiator… Everyone negotiates something every day,” wrote Roger Fisher, William Ury, and Bruce Patton in their foundational negotiation book,
Getting to Yes.
So, what do our many negotiations have in common? And what tools help us negotiate more effectively—whether the stakes are large or small?
What Is
Negotiation
?
Negotiation can be defined in several complementary ways.
In
Getting to Yes
, Fisher, Ury, and Patton describe negotiation as “back-and-forth communication designed to reach an agreement when you and the other side have some interests that are shared and others that are opposed.”
Similarly, Leigh Thompson, author of
The Mind and Heart of the Negotiator
, calls negotiation an “interpersonal decision-making process” that is necessary whenever we cannot achieve our objectives on our own.
And in
Judgment in Managerial Decision Making
, Max H. Bazerman and Don A. Moore explain that “when two or more parties need to reach a joint decision but have different preferences, they negotiate.”
Taken together, these definitions highlight three core features of negotiation:
Interdependence
– You need to work with another party or parties to reach your goals.
Mixed interests
– Some of your goals overlap with those of the other party or parties, while others conflict.
Communication
– Dialogue is required to reach an agreement or resolution.
The Seven Elements of Negotiation
Most people are not
natural-born negotiators
. The good news? Research shows that we can improve our
negotiation skills
dramatically with preparation and practice.
Members of the Harvard Negotiation Project developed the
Seven Elements framework
to help negotiators prepare more effectively for
negotiation
and reduce unwanted surprises. These seven elements provide a practical roadmap for creating and claiming value.
Here’s an overview:
Interests
Interests
are the fundamental drivers of negotiation—your underlying needs, desires, fears, and motivations. If our positions are what we say we want, then our interests are why we want it.
To identify interests, skilled negotiators ask probing questions, look beyond surface demands, and identify shared and differing priorities. Understanding interests opens the door to creative solutions.
Legitimacy
In negotiation, we don’t just care about our objective outcomes. Our fairness perceptions also matter to us a great deal.
Even if a deal benefits you financially or otherwise, you may reject it if it feels unfair.
Legitimacy
refers to objective standards or norms that justify proposals—market value, precedent, expert opinion, industry benchmarks.
To strengthen the legitimacy of a proposal or offer, you can:
Use independent standards,
Cite comparable agreements, and
Frame proposals around fairness.
When both sides feel respected and fairly treated, your agreements will become more durable.
Relationships
Relatedly, another important goal of negotiation is to build lasting relationships.
Whether you are negotiating with a long-term client, a colleague, a supplier, or someone you’ve just met, how you
manage the relationship
affects your reputation, trust between the parties, and future opportunities.
You can form strong relationships in negotiation by taking time to build rapport, upholding your ethical standards, and keeping disagreements civil and respectful.
BATNA: Strong Outside Alternatives
In a given negotiation, your
BATNA—Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement
—is your backup plan.
As emphasized in
Getting to Yes
, cultivating a strong BATNA gives you power in your current negotiation. If you have strong alternatives, you negotiate from a position of confidence. If your alternatives are weak, you may feel pressured to agree to a deal that doesn’t meet your interests.
Your BATNA analysis should include:
Identifying realistic alternatives,
Improving them where possible, and
Estimating your counterpart’s BATNA.
Options
Options
are possible solutions that satisfy both or all parties’ interests.
Rather than arguing over a single issue, like price, experienced negotiators:
Add new issues to the mix,
Look for trades across their different interests,
Propose contingent agreements based on possible future outcomes, and
Brainstorm all the issues without making firm commitments until they have all been discussed.
Commitments
A commitment
is a promise, agreement, or formal obligation. Commitments can range from the informal, like “Let’s meet Tuesday at 10” to a written proposal or signed contract.
Clear commitments reduce misunderstanding and conflict. Therefore, effective negotiators ensure commitments are:
Specific
Realistic
Mutually understood
Documented when possible
Communication
Every negotiation is a communication process—whether face-to-face, by phone,
email, or video
. Our communication choices shape our outcomes in negotiation. Threats and demands can derail talks, while inquiries and active listening build understanding and agreement. Skilled negotiators ask questions, paraphrase what they heard, and check their assumptions.
Why Preparation Matters
The Seven Elements framework shifts focus in negotiation from improvisation to preparation.
Before negotiating, ask yourself:
What are my interests?
What might their interests be?
What is my BATNA?
What might be the other party’s BATNA?
What legitimacy standards apply?
What options could create value?
How will I manage the relationship?
What commitments am I prepared to make?
What is the best communication medium for this stage of the negotiation?
Armed with this preparation, you are better positioned to:
Create value
Claim value
Address fairness concerns
Reach sustainable agreements
Negotiation is not reserved for diplomats and CEOs. It’s a daily skill—one that improves with intention.
