 20 Years of the Open Budget Survey shows gains but accountability gaps remain - International Budget Partnership Press Release 20 Years of the Open Budget Survey shows gains but accountability gaps remain Jun 23, 2026 The latest Open Budget Survey and its data over twenty years reveals that governments are publishing budget information but the public still cannot see whether money was spent as promised, how fiscal risks are managed, whether oversight institutions acted on what they found, or whether public input shaped decisions. Global budget transparency scores have increased 33 % since 2008 and almost every region in the world has made gains in the last ten rounds of the survey. Nevertheless, disclosure is not enough. Accountability requires greater capacity and space for the public and oversight institutions to engage with and act on budget information. Here important gaps remain. Governments disclose far less information during budget execution than during formulation. Only 41% of assessed countries produce Mid-Year Reviews. Scores for all pillars measured drop during the execution stage which is when governments are most likely to shift funds. These are precisely the moments where chronic underspending against approved budgets is most common, especially in social sectors that often get deprioritized, such as in health and education. Governments also provide too little information on how they’re raising and borrowing public funds. Of the 82 countries assessed in the Open Budget Survey 2025, 55% disclose nothing on tax expenditures, 50% provide no debt composition data, and 82% give no long-term fiscal outlook. Legislators have less time than ever to review budget proposals and weaker oversight during execution stages. Audit findings also often go unaddressed. More than half of governments ask communities for input on their budgets, but only 8% ever tell them how their input was used. Despite these gaps there are bright spot countries that have shown reform is possible anywhere. Benin and Dominican Republic have made the largest budget transparency gains in (76 points since 2012 and 70 points since 2008 respectively) and bolstered substantially their participation practices. Guatemala maintained gains through years of political crisis and rebounded sharply under the new leadership this past year. Cambodia, Cameroon, Egypt, Jordan and Kyrgyz Republic have also made notable progress. What unites the most promising reform paths are institutional anchors, technically capable budget offices and active civil society that sustains demand through political pressure and solutions-building—allowing fiscal reforms to take place even in challenging broader political contexts. Recommendations: Transparency — Governments should disclose more information during the execution stage as well as debt terms, tax expenditures, arrears, and state-owned enterprise risks. Participation —Governments should engage communities throughout the budget cycle (including during execution) and report back on how their input was used. Oversight — Legislators and auditors need support to reclaim legislative scrutiny of in-year spending shifts and to ensure audit findings have executive follow-through. Donors should invest in the full accountability ecosystem — government reformers, legislatures, auditors, civil society, and media — working together, not in isolation to ensure public money serves public interests. About the Open Budget Survey First launched in 2006, the Open Budget Survey (OBS), produced by the International Budget Partnership, is the world’s only independent, comparative, and fact-based research instrument to measure national budget accountability. It assesses each country across three dimensions: transparency of budget information, opportunities for meaningful public participation, and the authority and practices of legislative and audit oversight. The 2025 edition covers 82 countries worldwide and is conducted by independent civil society researchers in each country.
