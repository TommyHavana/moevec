Executive Summary
1 This year, 262 colleges and universities were surveyed. One school, Regis University, was given a Warning rating for its speech policies and 
was therefore excluded from the rankings. As a result, the 2027 College Free Speech Rankings includes 261 colleges and universities.
For the seventh year in a row, the Foundation for Individual 
Rights and Expression (FIRE), a nonprofit organization 
committed to defending and sustaining the individual 
rights of all Americans to free speech and free thought, 
and College Pulse surveyed college undergraduates about 
their perceptions and experiences regarding free speech 
on their campuses.
This year’s survey includes 62,997 student respondents 
from 2621 colleges and universities. Students enrolled in 
four-year degree programs were surveyed via the College 
Pulse mobile app and web portal from Jan. 2 through 
June 3, 2026.
Overall, the findings suggest some improvement in cam-
pus free speech climates, while also showing that many 
students still report difficulty discussing controversial 
topics and remain skeptical of allowing controversial 
speakers on campus.
The College Free Speech Rankings are available online 
and are presented in an interactive dashboard that allows 
for easy comparison between institutions. The full pre-
registered methodology is available on the Open Science 
Framework website.
KEY FINDINGS:
 
■
The University of Chicago ranks first for freedom of 
speech for the third time in seven years. Claremont 
McKenna College, last year’s top-ranked school, 
ranks second, followed by the Georgia Institute of 
Technology, Michigan Technological University, and 
Vanderbilt University. 
 
■
New York University ranks last. The University 
of Washington, Texas State University,  
Northeastern University, and Rutgers  
University–New Brunswick round out the  
bottom five. 
 
 
■
This year, the average overall score (61.36) is 
the highest recorded in seven years, though  
it still corresponds to a D- on the report’s  
grading scale. Overall, 121 schools still receive 
an F, while just 24 receive a C or better. 
 
■
The University of California, Davis had the  
largest year-over-year increase in its overall 
score and now ranks 24, compared with  
252 last year. Other schools with notable score  
increases include Harvard University, Barnard 
College, and Columbia University. 
 
■
In contrast, Texas State University had the  
largest year-over-year decrease in its overall 
score and now ranks 259, down from 112th last 
year. Other schools with considerable score 
decreases include Texas A&M University, the 
University of Arkansas, Utah Valley University, 
New York University, and Clemson University. 
 
■
Half of students surveyed said that the Israeli- 
Palestinian conflict was difficult to discuss openly 
and honestly on campus, down from 53% last year. 
Notable portions of students also identified Donald 
Trump (46%), Charlie Kirk (44%), abortion (43%), 
immigration (38%), and transgender rights (38%) 
as difficult topics to discuss. 
 
■
The percentage of students who said it was  
acceptable to shout down a speaker, block  
entry to a campus speech, or use violence to 
stop a campus speech declined slightly from 
last year’s record highs. 
 
■
For the second year in a row, a majority of  
students opposed allowing each of the six  
controversial speakers included in the survey  
— three conservative and three liberal — to 
speak on campus.

In 2020, in collaboration with College Pulse and Real-
ClearEducation, the Foundation for Individual Rights and 
Expression (FIRE) launched a first-of-its-kind tool to help 
high school students and their parents identify which 
colleges promote and protect the free exchange of ideas: 
the College Free Speech Rankings. Over the past seven 
years, the annual rankings survey has captured the voices 
of almost 350,000 students — and over 60,000 this year 
alone. Whether you’re a prospective student sizing up your 
options, a professor curious about classroom openness, 
or an administrator charting a path forward, FIRE’s 2027 
College Free Speech Rankings offer a data-driven look at 
where campus free speech is flourishing — and where it 
is failing.
As in previous years, the College Free Speech Rankings 
dashboard offers a unique tool to compare schools’ rank-
ings and survey responses and also explore other factors 
— such as cost and proximity to home.
This report begins by presenting this year’s rankings, high-
lighting the top-performing schools and detailing why 
schools near the bottom perform poorly.2 It then briefly 
discusses schools whose rankings improved or declined 
significantly over the past year before turning to an analysis 
of national trends that have emerged among undergraduate 
students over the past seven years.
HOW THE RANKINGS ARE CALCULATED
Each school’s overall score is the sum of 12 components. 
Six measure student perceptions, three assess campus 
speech policies, and three evaluate how schools respond 
to speech controversies. Higher scores indicate a better 
climate for free speech and expression.
 
 THE SIX SURVEY-BASED COMPONENTS ARE:
 
■
“Comfort Expressing Ideas” measures how com-
fortable students are expressing controversial 
views in five campus settings. 
 
■
“Self-Censorship” measures how often students 
withhold their views in conversations with 
students, professors, and administrators, and 
during classroom discussions. 
 
■
“Disruptive Conduct” measures students’  
acceptance of shouting down speakers,  
blocking access to events, and using violence  
to stop campus speech. 
 
■
“Administrative Support” measures whether 
students believe administrators clearly protect 
free speech and would defend a speaker during 
a controversy. 
 
■
“Openness” measures how many of 22 social 
and political topics students identify as difficult 
to discuss openly and honestly. 
 
■
“Political Tolerance” measures students’ will-
ingness to allow six controversial speakers on 
campus — three offensive to liberals and three 
to conservatives. The score rewards overall tol-
erance and tolerance across ideological lines. 
The three policy components are the Chicago 
Statement, which recognizes an explicit commitment to 
free expression; Institutional Neutrality, which recognizes 
a commitment to avoid taking positions on social and 
political issues unrelated to a university’s mission; and 
Spotlight Rating, which evaluates written policies regulating 
student expression.

The release of the 2027 College Free Speech Rankings 
bookends a jarring year for free expression on campus. 
One day after the release of last year’s rankings, conser-
vative activist and Turning Point USA founder Charlie Kirk 
was assassinated at Utah Valley University, doing what he 
often did: engaging in open political debate.4 His assas-
sination had a profound impact on students nationwide. 
A FIRE survey of 2,028 college undergraduates conducted 
last October found that half of the students surveyed felt 
less comfortable attending or hosting controversial public 
events on their campus than before Kirk’s assassination. 
About half also said they were less comfortable expressing 
their views on controversial topics in class, in common 
campus spaces, and on social media. One in five even said 
they were less comfortable attending class in person.5
Charlie Kirk’s assassination also prompted increased pres-
sure from public officials to sanction faculty speech, primar-
ily in response to social media comments about his death.6 
For example, Indiana Attorney General Todd Rokita 
launched an Eyes on Education platform, which is “de-
signed to give parents, students, and educators a way to 
share examples of what is being taught and promoted in 
schools and to provide transparency for parents and keep 
Hoosiers informed.”7 Multiple submissions to this platform 
concern comments made by college and university faculty 
about Kirk’s political views and his assassination. Alabama’s 
U.S. Sen. Tommy Tuberville took a more direct approach, 
demanding that the Huntsville and Tuscaloosa campuses of 
the University of Alabama fire multiple professors for their 
comments about Kirk’s death on social media.8
Elsewhere, Clemson University condemned the expression 
of two professors — Joshua Bregy and Melvin Earl Villaver 
Jr. — who made controversial social media posts in re-
sponse to Kirk’s death, but initially resisted punishing them 
while emphasizing its commitment to the First Amendment. 
After U.S. Rep. Nancy Mace and South Carolina Attorney 
General Alan Wilson amplified public backlash, Clemson 
reversed course and decided to terminate both profes-
sors.9 Similarly, the University of Tennessee capitulated 
to demands from state and federal lawmakers to fire pro-
fessor Tamar Shirinian because of a social media post she 
made following Kirk’s assassination.10 While at Fort Hays 
State University, professor Nuchelle Chance was placed 
on administrative leave after the Kansas Republican Party 
condemned her social media comments and demanded 
that the university terminate her.11
Other on-campus censorship efforts focused on a 
different target: Turning Point USA, the organization 
Charlie Kirk founded and led. These efforts came primarily 
from student governments. Attempts to establish TPUSA

THE BEST AND WORST-RANKED  
SCHOOLS FOR FREEDOM OF SPEECH
For the third time in seven years, the University of Chicago 
is the top-ranked school for free speech, with an overall 
score of 81.42 and a speech climate grade of B-. Claremont 
McKenna College (80.13), last year’s top-ranked school, is 
ranked second. The Georgia Institute of Technology (78.98), 
Michigan Technological University (78.72), and Vanderbilt 
University (77.09) round out the top five. Like UChicago, 
CMC has a speech climate grade of B-, while the rest of 
the schools ranked in the top five have a C+.
At the other end of the spectrum, New York University 
ranks last, with an overall score of 44.97. This comes 
after finishing as the third-worst school last year. The 
University of Washington (47.50), Texas State University 
(47.82), Northeastern University (49.78), and Rutgers 
University (49.91) round out the bottom five. The University 
of Washington and Northeastern were both ranked in the 
bottom five last year as well. All of these schools receive 
a failing grade of F for their speech climate. 
The contrasts between the best and worst schools 
are stark. 
The average overall score of schools in the top five is 79.27, 
compared with an average of 47.99 among the bottom five 
schools — a difference of almost five standard deviations. 
Furthermore, the average ranking across the different 
survey-based components among the top five was 47 
— including an average ranking of 12 on “Administrative 
Support” and 16 on “Political Tolerance.” The lowest average 
ranking among these schools on any of the survey-based 
components was 79 on “Disruptive Conduct.” 
Things are considerably different at schools in the bottom 
five, whose average ranking on the survey-based compo-
nents is 178 — including 210 on “Administrative Support” 
and 211 on “Disruptive Conduct.” The only survey-based 
component on which schools in the bottom five rank above 
the median is “Openness,” with an average ranking of 101.

The story is similar when comparing the top five and 
bottom five on the behavioral metrics of the rankings. 
All of the top five schools have a “green light” rating for 
their speech policies27 and have endorsed the Chicago 
Statement issued in 2015.28 Four of these five schools — 
Michigan Tech being the exception — have also adopted 
a commitment to institutional neutrality.29 Additionally, 
none of them incurred any penalties for speech contro-
versies on their campuses.
In contrast, the bottom five schools all lost points due to 
their speech policies and the outcomes of speech con-
troversies that occurred on their campuses. None of the 
schools have endorsed the Chicago Statement or adopted 
a stance of institutional neutrality. 
Northeastern’s speech policies earn a “red light” rating.30 
The remaining schools in the bottom five all have “yellow 
light” ratings.31 Worse, these schools collectively lost 41 
points due to the outcomes of different speech contro-
versies on their campuses.32
The University of Chicago and Claremont McKenna College 
earned the two highest scores ever: 81.42 and 80.13, re-
spectively. This is encouraging, but those scores are also 
equivalent to a grade of B-. The average score among the 
top five schools is a C+ (79.27), or slightly “above average.” 
So, even the best schools for free speech have plenty of 
work still to do to create a truly open environment for ex-

emerged: Students are reluctant to speak their minds,

two highest scores ever, 81.42 and 80.13, respectively, and 
both scores correspond to a B-. The average score among 
the top five schools is a C+ (79.27). Thus, even the “best” 
schools for free speech still have plenty of work to do to 
create a truly open environment for expression on their 
campuses. When the scope is expanded to all of the 261 
schools ranked this year, just 24 receive a C or better, and 
121 of them still receive a failing grade. 
These results are not solely a reflection of college poli-
cies or administrators. Campus speech climates are also 
shaped by broader cultural forces, including political 
polarization, intolerance of disagreement, and fear of 
social consequences. Colleges cannot fully control the 
attitudes students bring to campus, but their policies, 
leadership, and responses to controversy can either 
strengthen or weaken the culture of free expression.
In other words, while there have been improvements at 
a number of schools, the overall state of affairs for free 
speech on American college and university campuses 
remains very concerning. 
Over the past seven years, it has become apparent that 
strong leadership at colleges and universities is needed 
to establish at the very least the basic conditions of an 
open expressive environment. Schools that clearly and 
consistently support First Amendment protections and 
do the best they can to establish a campus environment 
that prioritizes open expression perform better in FIRE’s 
College Free Speech Rankings. This comes not just from 
sending messages to students and faculty on campus that 
the school values free and open expression, but also from 
consistently demonstrating that they mean what they say 
on this issue when controversy inevitably erupts.
We do not think Harvard’s improvement this year — it 
ranked 245 last year after finishing dead last in the 2024 
and 2025 rankings — is a coincidence.53 Its new leadership 
has made its support of the First Amendment clear.54 The 
school’s rise in the rankings is the result of a significant 
improvement in its score on “Administrative Support,” 
the decay of penalties imposed for older campus speech 
controversies, and the absence of new penalties for 
new controversies.
In many ways, Harvard’s story is similar to those of other 
schools over the past seven years. DePauw University, 
the University of South Carolina, Vanderbilt University, 
Dartmouth College, and others have reformed their 
policies and then, when faced with controversy, 
continued to stand firm in their reinvigorated support for the 
First Amendment.
This is the way. 
We hope more colleges and universities start to agree 
with us in the coming years.

About us
COLLEGE PULSE
College Pulse is a survey research and analytics company 
dedicated to understanding the attitudes, preferences, 
and behaviors of today’s college students. College Pulse 
delivers custom data-driven marketing and research 
solutions, utilizing its unique American College Student 
Panel™ that includes over 1,000,000 college students 
and recent alumni from more than 1,500 two- and four-
year colleges and universities in all 50 states. 
For more information,  
visit collegepulse.com or @CollegeInsights on X.
FIRE
The Foundation for Individual Rights and Expression 
(FIRE) is a nonpartisan, nonprofit organization dedicated 
to defending and sustaining the individual rights of all 
Americans to free speech and free thought. These rights 
include freedom of speech, freedom of association, due 
process, legal equality, religious liberty, and sanctity of 
conscience — the most essential qualities of liberty. 
FIRE also recognizes that colleges and universities play a 
vital role in preserving free thought within a free society. 
To this end, we place a special emphasis on defending 
these rights of students and faculty members on our 
nation’s campuses.
For more information,  
visit fire.org or @thefireorg on X.
